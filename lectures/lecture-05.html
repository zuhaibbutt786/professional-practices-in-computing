Module 05 · IP & Community

Intellectual Property, Licensing & Open Source

Copyright, patents, trade secrets, software licenses, open-source obligations, and the professional responsibilities of developers who create, reuse, and distribute software.

⏱ ~90–120 min📚 Law + practice🎯 IP & OSS
🎯

Learning Objectives

  • Distinguish copyright, patents, trademarks, and trade secrets as they apply to software and data.
  • Explain the purpose and basic terms of common software licenses (proprietary, permissive, copyleft).
  • Identify professional duties when reusing third-party code and when contributing to open source.
  • Discuss ethical and practical issues around patents in software and defensive vs. aggressive patent strategies.
  • Recognize license compatibility and attribution requirements in real projects.
📜

Intellectual Property Basics

Copyright protects original expression (source code, documentation, UI design in many jurisdictions) automatically upon fixation. It does not protect ideas, algorithms, or facts.

Patents can protect inventions that meet novelty, non-obviousness, and utility requirements; software-related patents remain controversial and jurisdiction-dependent.

Trade secrets protect confidential business information that derives value from secrecy and is subject to reasonable secrecy measures.

Trademarks protect brand identifiers and reduce consumer confusion.

📄

Software Licenses

Licenses grant permission to use, copy, modify, and distribute under specified conditions. Proprietary licenses typically restrict redistribution and modification. Open-source licenses form a spectrum:

  • Permissive (MIT, Apache 2.0, BSD) — few restrictions; attribution usually required; compatible with proprietary products.
  • Copyleft / reciprocal (GPL family) — modifications and derivative works distributed further must use a compatible copyleft license.
  • Weak copyleft (LGPL, MPL) — intermediate obligations focused on the library or file level.
Professional practice

Always check and record the licenses of every dependency. License conflicts and missing attribution create legal and reputational risk. “I found it on the internet” is not a defense.

🌐

Open Source as Professional Practice

Open source is both a licensing model and a set of collaboration norms. Professionals who contribute should respect project codes of conduct, contribution guidelines, and the project’s license. Organizations that consume open source have duties to comply with licenses, support critical maintainers where appropriate, and avoid treating volunteer maintainers as free labor for critical infrastructure.

✅

Knowledge Check

Using a dependency under the MIT license typically requires:
Releasing your entire product under the GPL.
Preserving copyright and license notices (attribution) as required by the license.
Paying royalties to the original author.
Making your source code public.
📋

Summary

  • IP law shapes what you can copy, modify, and distribute; understand the category that applies.
  • License compliance is a professional obligation, not optional paperwork.
  • Open source creates shared infrastructure; reciprocity and respect for maintainers matter.
