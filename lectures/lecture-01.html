Lecture 01 · Module 1

Introduction to Professional Practices in Computing

Why professionalism matters, what it means to be a computing professional, and how this course prepares you for ethical, legal, and societal challenges across CS, SE, Data Science, AI, and Cybersecurity.

Professional Identity Ethics Responsibility Career

Learning Objectives

By the end of this lecture you will be able to:

  • Define what professional practice means in computing and contrast it with purely technical skill.
  • Explain why computing professionals carry special responsibilities toward users, organizations, and society.
  • Identify the main stakeholder groups affected by computing systems and the types of harm or benefit that can arise.
  • Describe the multi-perspective lens (ethical, legal, business, AI, data, SE, security) used throughout this course.
  • Locate yourself on a professional development roadmap and set initial goals for continuous learning.

Motivation — Why This Course Exists

Software, data, and AI systems now mediate almost every aspect of modern life: healthcare decisions, credit scoring, hiring, criminal justice risk assessment, autonomous vehicles, content recommendation, and national infrastructure. When these systems fail or are misused, the consequences are not abstract — people lose jobs, privacy, freedom, health, or even life.

Universities have traditionally taught technical skills extremely well and professional judgment less systematically. This course closes that gap. It is deliberately technology-independent: the principles of responsibility, accountability, fairness, transparency, and care apply whether you write Python, design ML pipelines, secure cloud workloads, or lead open-source projects.

Key idea: Technical excellence without professional judgment is incomplete. Professional judgment without technical understanding is ineffective. You need both.

Real Story — The Cost of Ignoring Professional Practice

In 2018, a self-driving test vehicle operated by a major technology company struck and killed a pedestrian in Arizona. Investigations later highlighted gaps in safety culture, testing protocols, operator monitoring, and the prioritization of deployment speed over rigorous validation. Engineers and managers faced difficult questions about whether the system was ready, whether edge cases had been adequately considered, and whether organizational incentives had discouraged raising safety concerns.

This was not a failure of algorithms alone. It was a failure of professional practice: risk assessment, escalation paths, ethical prioritization of human safety, and clear accountability. Similar patterns appear in medical device software recalls, biased hiring algorithms, data breaches caused by weak security culture, and content moderation systems that amplify harm.

Every computing professional will eventually face a moment when the "easy" technical path conflicts with the responsible path. This course trains you to recognize those moments and act with integrity.

Industrial Scenario

You are a mid-level engineer on a team building a recommendation system for a large social platform. Product leadership wants to maximize engagement metrics. Your analysis shows that the current ranking objective correlates with increased polarization and time spent on emotionally charged content. A junior colleague privately asks whether the team should raise this with leadership or quietly continue optimizing the metric.

Questions you should be able to answer after this course:

  • What professional and ethical obligations apply here?
  • Who are the stakeholders beyond the product metrics dashboard?
  • What channels exist for raising concerns, and what documentation should you keep?
  • How do ACM/IEEE codes and company policy interact in this situation?
  • What would "responsible" design look like if engagement were balanced against well-being?

Multi-Perspective Analysis

Ethical

What duties do we owe users, society, and future generations? How do we balance autonomy, non-maleficence, justice, and beneficence in system design?

Legal

Which laws, regulations, and liability frameworks apply? Privacy statutes, product liability, discrimination law, and emerging AI regulation all constrain choices.

Business

How do professional decisions affect reputation, risk, talent retention, and long-term value? Short-term metrics vs. sustainable trust.

AI / Data Science

Bias, explainability, data provenance, model governance, and the special responsibilities that come with probabilistic systems that affect people at scale.

Software Engineering

Requirements, validation, safety-critical practices, documentation, and the professional obligation to produce systems that are fit for purpose.

Cybersecurity

Security as a professional duty, not an afterthought. Threat modeling, responsible disclosure, and the ethics of offensive and defensive work.

Summary

  • Computing professionals influence systems that affect human welfare at scale.
  • Professional practice combines technical competence with ethical reasoning, legal awareness, and organizational judgment.
  • This course trains a multi-perspective approach and concrete decision skills usable across CS, SE, Data Science, AI, and Cybersecurity careers.
  • Next lectures examine the history of the profession, ethical theories, professional codes, and responsibility/accountability frameworks.
