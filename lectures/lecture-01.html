Lecture 01 — Introduction to Professional Practices in Computing

Why professionalism matters • Professional identity • Multi-perspective reasoning • Course roadmap

Learning Objectives

  • Articulate why technical skill alone is insufficient for professional computing practice.
  • Define the dual role of computing professionals as Creators and Stewards.
  • Identify the major dimensions of professional practice covered in this course.
  • Apply multi-perspective analysis (ethical, legal, business, AI, data, SE, security) to a simple scenario.
  • Recognize historical lessons from safety-critical system failures.

Introduction & Motivation

Computing systems now mediate almost every aspect of modern life: healthcare, finance, transportation, communication, education, and public safety. When these systems fail—or when they succeed in ways that harm people—the consequences are no longer limited to “bugs” or “outages.” They become questions of professional responsibility.

This course exists because the technical competence that lets you build powerful systems is not the same competence required to decide whether, how, and for whom those systems should be built. Professional Practices in Computing trains the second competence.

Real Story: Therac-25 and the Cost of Incomplete Professional Judgment

In the mid-1980s the Therac-25 radiation therapy machine delivered massive overdoses to several patients, killing some and severely injuring others. The root causes were not merely coding errors. They included over-reliance on software for safety interlocks, inadequate testing of rare race conditions, poor documentation, and an organizational culture that dismissed early incident reports.

The lesson is not “software is dangerous.” The lesson is that professional practice requires treating safety, verification, organizational process, and user impact as first-class design concerns—equal in importance to functional correctness.

Industrial Scenario

You are a junior engineer on a team shipping a feature that uses customer location data to personalize recommendations. Product wants the feature live in two weeks. Legal has not yet reviewed the privacy implications for the new markets being targeted. The data pipeline already exists and “just works.” What do you do?

Professional practice is the ability to slow the conversation, surface the missing perspectives (privacy, consent, regulatory exposure, long-term trust), and propose a path that balances speed with responsibility rather than treating them as opposites.

Core Concept: The Dual Role — Creator and Steward

Every computing professional occupies two roles simultaneously:

  • Creator — designs, implements, tests, and deploys systems.
  • Steward — considers the effects of those systems on people, organizations, society, and the environment, and takes responsibility for mitigating foreseeable harms.

Neither role is optional. A pure Creator who ignores impact produces systems that later generate scandals, lawsuits, or regulatory crackdowns. A pure Steward without technical depth cannot evaluate trade-offs or propose workable alternatives.

This course trains you to examine every significant decision through multiple lenses:

Legal
Business

No single perspective is sufficient. Real professional judgment is the integration of these views under time and resource constraints.

Professional Perspective

Professionalism is not a soft skill bolted onto technical work. It is the disciplined application of judgment about risk, stakeholders, evidence, and long-term consequences. ACM and IEEE codes exist because the profession itself recognizes that technical power creates obligations.

Ethical Perspective

Ethical analysis asks: Who is affected? What harms and benefits are foreseeable? Are rights or duties being violated? Is the distribution of benefits and burdens fair? Different ethical theories (utilitarian, deontological, virtue, care) illuminate different aspects of the same decision.

Legal Perspective

Law sets minimum standards and creates liability. Privacy regulation, intellectual property, product liability, and emerging AI rules shape what is permissible. Professionals must know when legal advice is required and when “technically legal” is still professionally unacceptable.

Business Perspective

Organizations exist to create value. Professional practice includes understanding incentives, risk appetite, reputation, and the difference between short-term metrics and sustainable trust. Many ethical failures begin as rational business decisions under incomplete information or misaligned incentives.

AI, Data Science, Software Engineering & Cybersecurity Perspectives

Each domain adds distinctive concerns: model opacity and bias (AI), consent and secondary use (data), maintainability and technical debt (SE), and threat modeling and residual risk (security). A mature professional can translate across these languages.

Knowledge Check

Reflection

  1. Describe a recent technical decision you made (or observed) and list at least three perspectives you did not fully consider at the time.
  2. In your own words, what does it mean to be a Steward as well as a Creator?
  3. Why might an organization that values speed still benefit from slower, multi-perspective review on high-impact features?

Summary

  • Technical excellence and professional judgment are complementary and both necessary.
  • Computing professionals act as Creators and Stewards.
  • Multi-perspective analysis is a core professional habit.
  • Historical failures teach that process, culture, and verification matter as much as individual skill.
  • This course builds the judgment needed for responsible practice across ethics, law, business, AI, data, software engineering, and security.
