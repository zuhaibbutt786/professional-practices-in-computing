Lecture 01

Introduction to Professional Practices

Why professionalism matters for computing graduates, the map of this course, and the identity of a computing professional.

Learning Objectives

  • Explain why professional practices are essential in computing careers.
  • Describe the scope of professional responsibility beyond writing correct code.
  • Identify the major themes of this course (ethics, law, AI, data, communication, career).
  • Articulate a first draft of your professional identity as a computing graduate.

Motivation

Technical skill alone is not enough. Computing systems shape health, finance, justice, democracy, and everyday life. When systems fail—or succeed in harmful ways—the impact is rarely limited to a single company. Employers, regulators, and the public increasingly expect computing professionals to exercise judgment about risk, fairness, privacy, safety, and social impact.

This course develops that judgment. It does not teach a particular language or framework. It teaches how to think as a professional: to see stakeholders, trade-offs, duties, and long-term consequences.

Real Story

In 2018, a widely used social platform faced intense scrutiny after data from millions of users was harvested and used for political targeting without adequate consent or transparency. Engineers who built data pipelines and APIs had made technical decisions under product pressure. Many later reflected that clearer professional norms, stronger internal challenge culture, and earlier escalation might have changed outcomes. The incident became a global case study in privacy, consent, and organizational responsibility.

Industrial Scenario

You join a startup building a recommendation system for a health-related consumer app. The product team wants maximum engagement. Your model can increase session time by surfacing emotionally charged content. A junior colleague asks whether engagement metrics should be the primary success measure when the domain is health. How do you respond as a professional—not only as an engineer?

Core Concepts

Profession vs. job. A job is paid work. A profession involves specialized knowledge, a commitment to the public interest, and standards that can constrain commercial pressure.

Stakeholders. Users, non-users affected by systems, employers, clients, regulators, society, and future generations.

Duty of care. A reasonable professional anticipates foreseeable harms and takes proportionate steps to prevent or mitigate them.

Accountability. Being answerable for decisions and outcomes, including the decision to remain silent.

Perspectives

Ethical: What should we do when interests conflict?

Legal: What are we required to do under law and regulation?

Business: What sustains trust and long-term value?

AI / Data Science: Models and data pipelines encode choices about whose interests count.

Software Engineering: Requirements, architecture, and process determine where ethical risk lives.

Cybersecurity: Security failures are often professional failures of prioritization and communication.

Knowledge Check

Reflection

Personal reflection

In one paragraph, describe the kind of computing professional you want to become. What values will you refuse to compromise?

Assignment

Write a 400–600 word professional identity statement. Include: (1) your intended role domain, (2) two professional values, (3) one risk area you want to understand deeply this term, (4) one concrete habit you will practice.

Summary

Professional practices turn technical ability into trustworthy service. This course builds ethical reasoning, legal awareness, communication, leadership, and judgment for a career that will outlast any single tool stack.
