Lecture 01

Introduction to Professional Practices in Computing

Core foundations · ~45–60 min · Case + Quiz + Reflection

Learning Objectives

  • Explain why professional practices matter beyond technical skill in computing careers.
  • Distinguish professional identity from purely technical competence.
  • Identify the major domains covered by this course (ethics, law, AI, privacy, communication, career).
  • Describe how ACM and IEEE codes shape expectations for computing professionals.
  • Reflect on your own emerging professional responsibilities.

Motivation: Why This Course Exists

Software, data systems, and AI now mediate almost every aspect of modern life—from healthcare and finance to public discourse and critical infrastructure. Technical excellence alone is no longer sufficient. Employers, regulators, and society expect computing graduates to exercise ethical judgment, communicate clearly, protect privacy and security, and take responsibility for the consequences of the systems they build.

This course is designed to build that professional judgment. It is technology-independent: the focus is on thinking, not on any particular language or framework.

Real Story: When Technical Skill Was Not Enough

In multiple high-profile incidents (e.g., privacy breaches, biased automated decisions, safety-critical software failures), teams with strong engineering talent still produced outcomes that harmed users, violated laws, or damaged trust. Post-mortems often point not only to technical gaps but to missing professional habits: inadequate risk discussion, weak escalation culture, unclear ownership of ethical trade-offs, and insufficient attention to affected stakeholders.

These cases show that “it compiles and ships” is not the same as “it is responsible.”

What “Professional Practices” Means Here

Professional practices in computing include:

  • Ethical reasoning — applying frameworks and codes to real dilemmas.
  • Legal and regulatory awareness — privacy, IP, safety, AI governance at a conceptual level.
  • Communication and teamwork — writing, presenting, collaborating, resolving conflict.
  • Responsibility and accountability — ownership of outcomes, escalation, documentation.
  • Career and lifelong learning — identity, branding, continuous skill development.

Industrial Scenario

You join a product team building a recommendation system used by millions. A stakeholder asks you to optimise only for engagement metrics. Early tests show the model amplifies polarising content. You have no formal “ethics officer” on the team. What do you do first? Who do you involve? How do you document the concern?

This course gives you structured ways to answer such questions under time pressure and organisational constraints.

Course Roadmap (High Level)

  7. Project risk, safety-critical systems, human factors

Professional Perspective

ACM and IEEE codes emphasise public interest, honesty, competence, and fairness. Professionalism means acting as a member of a community that holds standards higher than “what the contract requires.”

Multi-Perspective Lens (Used Throughout the Course)

Every major topic will be examined from multiple angles:

  • Ethical
  • Legal
  • Business / organisational
  • AI / data science
  • Software engineering
  • Cybersecurity

This habit of multi-perspective analysis is itself a professional skill.

Knowledge Check (Quiz)

Reflection

In 150–250 words, describe one situation (real or hypothetical) where technical success alone would not have been enough. What professional practices would have improved the outcome?

Assignment Preview

After completing this lecture, complete the short written exercise linked from the Assignments page (or provided by your instructor): map your current skills against the professional competencies listed in the learning outcomes and identify two areas for deliberate practice this term.

Summary & Cheat Sheet

  • Professional practice = ethical judgment + responsibility + communication + continuous learning.
  • Technical skill is necessary but not sufficient.
  • Use multi-perspective analysis on every significant decision.
  • Align daily work with ACM/IEEE-style public-interest obligations.

Further Reading

  • IEEE Code of Ethics
  • Baase, A Gift of Fire (selected chapters on social issues)
  • Quinn, Ethics for the Information Age (introductory chapters)
