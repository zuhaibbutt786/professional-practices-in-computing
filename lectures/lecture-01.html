Module 01

Introduction to Professional Practices in Computing

Professionalism is not an optional soft skill. It is the disciplined capacity to design, build, and operate computing systems while protecting people, organizations, and society from foreseeable harm.

Learning Objectives

  • Define professional practices in the context of CS, Software Engineering, Data Science, AI, and Cybersecurity.
  • Explain why ACM and IEEE treat ethical and professional competence as core to the computing disciplines.
  • Apply a multi-perspective lens (ethical, legal, business, AI, data, SE, security) to a real decision.
  • Describe the structure, pedagogy, and expected outcomes of this course.
  • Articulate personal professional identity and the obligations that come with it.

Motivation — Why This Course Exists

Every major computing failure of the last two decades has roots in failures of professional judgment, not merely missing technical skill. Privacy scandals, biased AI systems, safety-critical software defects, and poorly governed data practices all share a common pattern: the people closest to the system either did not see the downstream consequences or did not feel empowered (or obligated) to act on them.

This course exists because technical excellence without professional responsibility is incomplete — and increasingly dangerous — in a world where software and data shape elections, health outcomes, credit decisions, employment, and public safety.

Real Story

When a major social platform optimized engagement metrics without sufficient regard for societal harm, the resulting information environment affected elections, public health discourse, and mental health at population scale. Engineers and product managers later reflected publicly that the professional obligation to consider downstream effects had been secondary to growth and retention targets.

The technical systems worked as designed. The professional systems — the norms, escalation paths, and ethical review processes — did not.

Industrial Scenario

You join a team building a recommendation system for a health-related consumer app. Early offline and online metrics show the model amplifies anxiety-inducing content for a vulnerable cohort of users. Business stakeholders are under pressure to hit engagement targets before the next board meeting.

Questions you must answer as a professional:

  • What evidence threshold is sufficient to pause or redesign the feature?
  • Who has the authority — and the duty — to escalate?
  • How do you document the risk so that future teams inherit the lesson?
  • What does "first, do no harm" mean when the harm is probabilistic and distributed?

Historical Background

Professionalization of computing accelerated after high-visibility failures (Therac-25, Ariane 5, London Ambulance, and later privacy and AI incidents). ACM and IEEE codes evolved from general statements of integrity into more specific obligations around public interest, privacy, discrimination, and system safety. Parallel developments in medicine, engineering, and aviation provide useful analogies — and warnings — about what happens when professions fail to enforce their own standards.

Core Concepts

Professional Identity

You are not only a coder, data scientist, or security analyst. You are a member of a profession that claims special knowledge and, in return, accepts special duties to clients, users, employers, and the public.

Multi-Perspective Analysis

Every significant decision is examined through ethical, legal, business, AI, data science, software engineering, and cybersecurity lenses. Single-perspective thinking is a professional risk.

Accountability

Professionals can explain what they did, why they did it, what alternatives they considered, and how they would act differently with better information.

Technology Independence

This course teaches professional thinking, not specific tools. The principles apply whether you work in systems, ML, security, product, or research.

Multi-Perspective Lens (Used Throughout the Course)

Ethical — duties, rights, consequences, virtues, justice
Legal — statutes, regulations, liability, compliance
Business — incentives, risk, reputation, sustainability
AI — fairness, transparency, robustness, human oversight
Data Science — consent, provenance, bias, secondary use
Software Engineering — requirements, safety, maintainability, process
Cybersecurity — confidentiality, integrity, availability, adversarial thinking

Examples Across Disciplines

  • Software Engineering: Choosing not to ship a feature with known accessibility barriers because "we'll fix it later" is a professional decision, not only a backlog decision.
  • Data Science: Training a model on data collected under one consent regime and deploying it under another raises professional as well as legal questions.
  • AI: Optimizing a ranking objective that correlates with engagement but also with polarization requires explicit professional judgment about acceptable trade-offs.
  • Cybersecurity: Disclosing a vulnerability to a vendor versus publishing full details involves balancing public benefit, user harm, and professional norms of responsible disclosure.

Knowledge Check

Professional practices in computing primarily concern:

  • Only writing correct and efficient code
  • Ethical reasoning, responsibility, communication, and societal impact alongside technical competence
  • Passing industry certification exams
  • Maximizing user engagement metrics

Reflection Questions

  1. Identify one decision you have already made (or observed) in a computing project that had ethical or professional dimensions. Which perspective was missing or under-weighted?
  2. If a junior colleague raises a concern about possible harm from a feature you own, what is your professional obligation in the first 24 hours?
  3. How would you explain to a non-technical executive why "the model is accurate" is not a sufficient answer when the model affects high-stakes decisions about people?

Summary

Professional Practices in Computing prepares you to act as a responsible computing professional. The course is technology-independent and focuses on judgment, codes of ethics, law, communication, leadership, and the societal impact of the systems you will design and operate. From this point forward, every module asks you to practice multi-perspective analysis before you conclude what "should" be done.
