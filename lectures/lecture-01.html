Module 01 · Week 1

Introduction to Professional Practices in Computing

Why professional practice matters in a world shaped by algorithms, data, and autonomous systems.

Reading time · ~45–60 min Level · Undergraduate

Learning Objectives

By the end of this module you will be able to:

  • Define professional practice in computing and distinguish it from technical skill alone.
  • Explain why computing professionals carry heightened responsibilities toward individuals, organizations, and society.
  • Identify the major domains of professional practice: ethics, law, privacy, security, AI responsibility, communication, teamwork, and career development.
  • Recognize real-world failures and successes that illustrate the cost of neglecting professional judgment.
  • Articulate a personal stance on what it means to be a responsible computing professional in 2026 and beyond.

1. Motivation: Why This Course Exists

Computing is no longer a niche technical craft. Software, data systems, and AI models now mediate hiring, credit, healthcare, mobility, public discourse, and national security. When these systems fail—or when they succeed in ways that harm—the consequences are measured in lives, livelihoods, and trust.

Traditional computer science curricula excel at teaching algorithms, architectures, and implementation. They often under-emphasize the professional judgment required to decide whether, how, and under what constraints a system should be built and deployed. This course closes that gap.

Professional Practices in Computing is technology-independent. It does not teach a programming language or a cloud console. It teaches the habits of mind, ethical frameworks, legal awareness, and communication skills that ACM, IEEE, and leading employers expect of graduates who will shape the digital world for the next decade.

2. Real Story: The Human Cost of Technical Decisions

Therac-25 (1985–1987)

A radiation therapy machine delivered massive overdoses to patients because of a race condition in the software and inadequate hardware interlocks. Operators trusted the system; engineers had removed hardware safety features relying on software that was never rigorously validated. Several patients died or suffered permanent injury.

Professional lesson: Safety-critical systems demand defense-in-depth. Software correctness is necessary but not sufficient; professional responsibility includes challenging design assumptions, insisting on independent verification, and refusing to ship when safety evidence is incomplete.

Boeing 737 MAX MCAS (2018–2019)

A flight-control system relied on a single angle-of-attack sensor. When that sensor failed, the system repeatedly pushed the nose down. Two crashes killed 346 people. Investigations revealed pressure to minimize training differences, limited disclosure to pilots and regulators, and organizational incentives that deprioritized safety culture.

Professional lesson: Organizational culture and commercial pressure can override engineering judgment. Professionals must cultivate the courage to escalate safety concerns and the institutional pathways that make escalation effective.

3. Industrial Scenario

You are a junior software engineer at a health-tech startup. Your team is racing to ship an AI triage assistant that prioritizes emergency-room patients. The model was trained primarily on data from urban hospitals in one country. A clinical advisor flags that rural and elderly populations are under-represented and that false negatives could delay critical care. The product manager argues that “we can improve the model after launch” and that competitors are already in market.

Discussion prompt: What professional obligations do you have? To whom? What concrete actions can a junior engineer take without being dismissed as “blocking the roadmap”?

4. What “Professional Practice” Means

A profession is more than a paid occupation. Professions typically involve specialized knowledge, a commitment to the public interest, codes of ethics, and accountability for judgment.

Key dimensions covered in this course: Ethics & Responsibility, Law & Regulation, Security & Privacy, AI & Data Responsibility, Communication & Teamwork, Career & Lifelong Learning.

5. Multi-Perspective Analysis

Professional, Ethical, Legal, Business, AI/Data Science, Software Engineering, and Cybersecurity perspectives must be considered together when evaluating technology decisions.

6. Knowledge Check & Reflection

Use the interactive quiz on the live site and the reflection prompts to consolidate learning. Technical skill enables building systems; professional practice governs whether, how, and under what constraints those systems should be built and deployed.

Summary

  • Computing systems mediate critical aspects of life; technical skill without professional judgment is incomplete.
  • Historical failures (Therac-25, 737 MAX) show safety, fairness, and accountability are design requirements.
  • This course develops professional thinking applicable across CS, SE, DS, AI, and cybersecurity roles.
