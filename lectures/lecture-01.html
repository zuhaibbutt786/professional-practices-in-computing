Introduction to Professional Practices in Computing

Why professionalism matters, what it means to be a computing professional, and how this course prepares you for ethical and responsible practice.

⏱ 75–90 min 📚 Core 🎯 Foundation ✍️ Reflection + Quiz
🎯

Learning Objectives

By the end of this lecture you will be able to:

  • Explain why professional practices are essential for computing graduates in CS, SE, DS, AI, and cybersecurity.
  • Distinguish between technical competence and professional competence.
  • Describe the dual role of a computing professional: creator of technology and steward of its societal impact.
  • Identify the major knowledge areas covered by this course and how they map to ACM/IEEE expectations.
  • Articulate a personal initial definition of “professionalism” in computing and refine it through reflection.
📖

Introduction

Computing is no longer a purely technical discipline practiced in isolation. Every line of code, every model trained, every system deployed, and every data pipeline carries consequences for individuals, organizations, and society. The power of modern computing — especially artificial intelligence, large-scale data systems, and interconnected infrastructure — has raised the stakes of professional judgment.

This course treats professionalism as a set of knowledge, skills, attitudes, and habits that enable computing graduates to act responsibly. It is not about memorizing rules. It is about developing the capacity to recognize ethical, legal, social, and organizational dimensions of technical work and to make reasoned decisions under uncertainty.

Core Premise
Technical excellence without professional judgment is incomplete. Professional judgment without technical understanding is ineffective. This course builds the bridge between the two.
🔥

Motivation — Why This Course Exists

Employers, accreditation bodies (ABET, ACM, IEEE), and society increasingly expect computing graduates to demonstrate more than coding ability. They expect graduates who can:

  • Recognize the broader impact of their work
  • Reason about privacy, fairness, safety, and security
  • Communicate clearly with non-technical stakeholders
  • Work effectively in diverse, often distributed teams
  • Continue learning as technology and norms evolve
  • Uphold professional standards even when incentives push the other way

Historical and contemporary failures — from software that contributed to loss of life, to large-scale privacy breaches, to biased algorithmic systems — repeatedly show that technical skill alone does not guarantee responsible outcomes. Professional practices courses exist to close that gap.

📰

Real Story — The Cost of Narrow Focus

In the late 2010s a major technology company rolled out a facial recognition system used by law-enforcement partners. The system performed significantly worse on certain demographic groups. Engineers who raised accuracy concerns internally were often told the priority was shipping features and meeting contractual milestones. Later public scrutiny, lawsuits, and policy debates forced the company to pause or restrict the technology in several jurisdictions.

The technical teams were highly skilled. The failure was not primarily a failure of algorithms; it was a failure of professional culture: insufficient attention to disparate impact, weak channels for raising concerns, and incentives that rewarded speed over careful evaluation of downstream harm.

This pattern — technical competence paired with incomplete professional judgment — appears across domains: autonomous vehicles, medical AI, content moderation systems, financial algorithms, and critical infrastructure software.

Industrial Scenario

Scenario: You Join a Fast-Growing Startup

You are a new graduate hired as a software engineer / data scientist at a Series-B startup building an AI-powered hiring platform. The product ranks candidates for recruiters using résumé data, online activity signals, and proprietary scoring models. Your first sprint includes improving model performance on a key client dataset. During exploration you notice that the model systematically ranks candidates from certain universities and demographic groups higher, even when controlling for experience metrics that appear job-relevant.

Questions to hold in mind (we will return to them):

  • Who are the stakeholders and what are their interests?
  • What professional responsibilities do you carry beyond “make the model more accurate”?
  • How would you raise the issue constructively?
  • What information would you need before recommending a change?

Historical Background — Computing Becomes a Profession

Computing began as a specialized technical activity and gradually acquired the characteristics of a profession: specialized knowledge, ethical codes, educational pathways, and a recognized social role.

1940s–1960s

Early computing largely embedded in scientific and military institutions. Professional identity still forming.

1960s–1970s

ACM and IEEE Computer Society grow. First formal discussions of ethics and social responsibility appear.

1990s

Software Engineering Code of Ethics (IEEE-CS/ACM) published. Recognition that software can cause significant harm.

2000s–2010s

Privacy regulation, open-source movement maturity, and large-scale internet platforms raise new professional questions.

2018–present

AI ethics, responsible AI frameworks (NIST, OECD, UNESCO), and heightened public scrutiny of algorithmic systems.

Today the profession is expected to integrate technical excellence with ethical awareness, legal literacy, and societal responsibility. This course reflects that expectation.

Key Concepts

What Is a Profession?

A profession is more than a job. Classic characteristics include: specialized knowledge requiring extended education; a commitment to serve a public good; self-regulation through codes and standards; and accountability for the consequences of one’s work.

Computing meets these criteria imperfectly but increasingly. Licensing is rare compared with medicine or civil engineering, yet codes of ethics, accreditation standards, and public expectations function as professional norms.

Technical Competence vs Professional Competence

Technical Competence

Ability to design, implement, test, and maintain systems correctly and efficiently. Mastery of algorithms, architectures, tools, and domain knowledge.

Professional Competence

Ability to situate technical work in ethical, legal, social, and organizational contexts; to communicate, collaborate, lead, and take responsibility for outcomes.

The Dual Role

Every computing professional occupies two roles simultaneously:

  • Creator — builds systems that solve problems and create value.
  • Steward — considers who is affected, how risks are distributed, and whether the system should be built or deployed in a given form.

Professional practice lives at the intersection of technical skill and professional judgment.

🔭

Multiple Perspectives on Professionalism

Professional practices look different depending on the lens you apply. This course trains you to switch lenses deliberately.

⚖️ Ethical

What values are at stake? Who benefits and who may be harmed? What would a fair process look like?

📜 Legal

What regulations, contracts, and liability regimes apply? What is the difference between legal compliance and ethical excellence?

💼 Business

How do incentives, metrics, and market pressures shape decisions? When does short-term gain create long-term risk?

🤖 AI

How do opacity, scale, and autonomous behavior change responsibility? What does meaningful human oversight require?

📊 Data Science

How do data provenance, representativeness, and downstream use affect legitimacy of insights and models?

🛠️ Software Engineering

How do process, architecture, testing, and documentation support or undermine professional accountability?

🔐 Cybersecurity

What duties arise from the ability to cause or prevent large-scale harm? How is responsible disclosure practiced?

Mini Case Study — Therac-25 (Historical Anchor)

The Therac-25 radiation therapy machine (1980s) delivered massive overdoses to several patients because of software race conditions and inadequate safety interlocks. Investigations revealed that the manufacturer had removed hardware safety mechanisms present in earlier models, relying instead on software, and that testing and quality practices were insufficient for a safety-critical system.

Professional lessons that remain relevant:

  • Safety-critical software demands different standards of evidence and review.
  • Removing physical safeguards without compensating analysis is a professional failure.
  • Organizational culture that prioritizes shipping over rigorous verification can produce catastrophic outcomes.
  • Engineers who sense risk need channels and norms that support escalation.

Later lectures will examine more recent cases (autonomous vehicles, large language models, data breaches, algorithmic hiring). Therac-25 remains a foundational reference for the cost of incomplete professional practice.

Assignment & Mini-Project

Assignment 1.1 — Professional Identity Statement (Individual)

Write a 400–600 word statement that answers:

  • What kind of computing professional do you aspire to become?
  • Which three professional competencies do you currently consider strongest, and which three need the most development?
  • Describe one concrete habit you will practice during this course to strengthen professional judgment.

Due: Before Lecture 3. Submit according to your instructor’s process (or keep in a personal portfolio).

Mini-Project Seed — Stakeholder Map

Choose any computing system you use regularly (social media feed, navigation app, university registration system, recommendation engine, etc.). Create a simple stakeholder map: list at least six stakeholder groups and, for each, note one potential benefit and one potential risk or harm. Bring the map to the next class or discussion session.

Summary

  • Real-world failures often stem from cultural and incentive problems as much as from technical ones.

Glossary

Professional competence
The integrated ability to apply technical knowledge while recognizing and acting on ethical, legal, social, and organizational responsibilities.
Stewardship
The responsibility to consider the long-term and collective consequences of technical decisions, not only immediate functional requirements.
Stakeholder
Any individual or group that can affect or is affected by a system or decision (users, non-users, operators, regulators, future generations, etc.).
Safety-critical system
A system whose failure or malfunction may result in death, serious injury, significant property damage, or environmental harm.
