Module 01

Introduction to Professional Practices in Computing

Foundations · Ethics · Responsibility · Career

Learning Objectives

  • Define professional practice in the context of computing disciplines
  • Explain why ethics, law, and social responsibility matter for CS, SE, DS, AI, and Cybersecurity graduates
  • Identify the major stakeholder groups affected by computing systems
  • Distinguish between personal morality, professional ethics, and organizational codes
  • Articulate the relationship between technical competence and professional responsibility

1. Motivation — Why This Course Exists

Computing professionals design systems that shape how people work, communicate, vote, receive healthcare, and make financial decisions. A single design choice can affect millions of lives. Technical skill alone is not enough; professionals must reason about consequences, trade-offs, and duties that extend beyond the immediate client or employer.

This course prepares you to act as a responsible professional: to recognize ethical issues early, to apply structured decision frameworks, to communicate clearly with non-technical stakeholders, and to contribute to a culture of integrity in teams and organizations.

2. Real Story — When Code Meets Consequence

Therac-25 (1985–1987)

A radiation therapy machine delivered massive overdoses to patients because of software race conditions and inadequate safety interlocks. At least six patients were seriously injured; several died. The incident remains a classic case study in the cost of treating software as an afterthought rather than a safety-critical system.

Professional lesson: Engineers who write code that can harm people carry a duty of care that goes beyond "it compiled and passed unit tests."

3. What Is a Computing Professional?

A professional is not merely someone who is paid for technical work. Professionalism implies:

  • Specialized knowledge that the public cannot fully evaluate
  • Autonomy in applying that knowledge
  • Accountability to clients, employers, the profession, and the public
  • Commitment to continuous learning and ethical standards

In computing, the boundary between "technician" and "professional" is often blurred because the field is young and barriers to entry are low. Codes of ethics (ACM, IEEE, BCS, and others) attempt to clarify the expectations that come with claiming professional status.

4. Multi-Perspective View

Software Engineering

Requirements, architecture, testing, and maintenance decisions all embed value judgments. Professional SE practice requires explicit attention to safety, security, and maintainability, not only feature delivery.

Data Science & AI

Models trained on biased data, deployed without impact assessment, or used for high-stakes decisions without human oversight create systemic risk. Responsible practice includes documentation, monitoring, and stakeholder engagement.

Cybersecurity

Security professionals often discover vulnerabilities or handle sensitive data. Ethical disclosure, dual-use awareness, and respect for privacy are core professional obligations.

Business & Legal

Organizations face regulatory pressure (GDPR, AI Act, sector-specific rules). Professionals who understand both technical constraints and legal/ethical obligations become valuable bridges between engineering and compliance.

5. Stakeholders and Impact

Every non-trivial system affects multiple parties:

  • End users and the public
  • Clients and employers
  • Colleagues and the profession
  • Society and future generations (sustainability, lock-in, path dependence)

Professional judgment involves balancing competing interests when they conflict. Simple rules ("always maximize profit" or "always protect privacy") fail in complex situations; structured ethical reasoning is required.

Summary

  • Professional practice in computing combines technical competence with ethical awareness, legal literacy, and accountability.
  • Historical failures (e.g., Therac-25) show the human cost of treating software as non-critical.
  • Multiple perspectives—SE, AI/DS, security, business/legal—must inform professional judgment.
  • Codes of ethics and decision frameworks exist to support consistent, defensible choices under uncertainty.
