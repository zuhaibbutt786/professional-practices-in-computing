Module 01 · Foundation

Introduction to Professional Practices in Computing

Why professionalism matters for every computer scientist, software engineer, data scientist, AI practitioner, and cybersecurity professional — and how ethical reasoning, codes of conduct, and societal impact shape the work you will do.

⏱ ~90–120 min 📚 Reading + cases + quiz 🎯 Foundations
🎯

Learning Objectives

  • Explain why professional practices matter beyond technical skill in computing careers.
  • Distinguish between personal morality, professional ethics, and organizational policy.
  • Summarize the purpose and structure of major professional codes (ACM, IEEE).
  • Apply a simple ethical decision framework to a realistic computing scenario.
  • Identify stakeholder impacts of computing decisions across technical, legal, business, and societal dimensions.
  • Reflect on your emerging professional identity as a computing student.
📖

Introduction

Computing is no longer a purely technical discipline confined to laboratories and code repositories. Algorithms allocate loans, recommend medical treatments, moderate speech, guide autonomous vehicles, and influence elections. The people who design, build, deploy, and maintain these systems carry responsibilities that extend far beyond correctness of code.

Professional Practices in Computing prepares you to reason about those responsibilities. This first module establishes the vocabulary, frameworks, and mindset you will use throughout the course and, more importantly, throughout your career.

Core idea

Technical competence is necessary but not sufficient. Professionalism is the disciplined practice of combining competence with ethical judgment, accountability, and concern for the people affected by technology.

💡

Motivation: Why This Course Exists

Industry and academia have repeatedly observed the same gap: graduates leave university with strong programming and analytical skills yet struggle when asked to navigate ethical dilemmas, communicate risk to non-technical stakeholders, or balance competing interests of users, employers, regulators, and society.

Employers, professional societies (ACM, IEEE), and accreditation bodies (ABET) explicitly require graduates to understand professional, ethical, legal, security, and social issues. This course is designed to meet and exceed those expectations with contemporary material spanning AI, data science, cybersecurity, software engineering, and digital society.

Consider the cost of treating professionalism as optional:

  • Systems that discriminate because of biased training data or poorly chosen metrics.
  • Security breaches that expose millions of records because convenience outweighed diligence.
  • Products rushed to market that later cause physical or financial harm.
  • Public distrust that slows beneficial innovation.

The reverse is also true: professionals who can articulate trade-offs, document decisions, and advocate for users become trusted leaders and create durable value.

📰

Real Story: The Therac-25 Accidents

In the mid-1980s, the Therac-25 radiation therapy machine delivered massive overdoses of radiation to several patients, resulting in severe injuries and deaths. Investigations revealed software race conditions, inadequate safety interlocks, poor error handling, and a culture that treated operator reports as user error rather than systemic signals.

Engineers and managers faced pressures of schedule, cost, and confidence in software over hardware safety mechanisms. The case remains a classic illustration that software in safety-critical systems demands professional practices — rigorous testing, independent review, clear accountability, and humility about what can go wrong — not merely clever code.

Professional lesson

When lives or significant harm are possible, "it works in most cases" is not an acceptable standard. Design for failure, listen to operators, and never assume software is inherently safer than the systems it replaces.

Industrial Scenario

You are a junior software engineer at a mid-size fintech company. Your team is shipping a new credit-scoring feature that uses alternative data (utility payments, mobile usage patterns) to expand access for customers with thin traditional credit files. Product wants the feature live before a competitor launches a similar offering. Legal has signed off on the high-level privacy notice. Your manager asks you to "just make the model a bit more aggressive on approval rates for the pilot."

Questions that a professional asks:

  • Who benefits and who may be harmed if the model is wrong?
  • Have we tested for disparate impact across protected groups?
  • Is the "aggressive" change documented, reviewed, and reversible?
  • What monitoring will detect drift or unexpected denial patterns after launch?
  • How will we explain a denial to a customer who asks?

Professional practice is the habit of raising these questions early, documenting answers, and escalating when answers are incomplete.

🔍

Case Study Snapshot: Cambridge Analytica & Facebook

Background. A researcher built a Facebook app that collected data not only from users who consented but also from their friends via the platform’s then-permissive API. That data later flowed to Cambridge Analytica and was used for political profiling and targeted messaging.

Ethical issues. Consent was incomplete and opaque. Users had little meaningful control or understanding of secondary uses. Platform design incentives prioritized growth and engagement over data minimization and purpose limitation.

Legal & business impact. Regulatory investigations, massive fines under emerging data-protection regimes, reputational damage, congressional hearings, and lasting erosion of public trust in social platforms and data-driven political advertising.

Professional lessons. Access control and API design are ethical decisions. "Users clicked accept" does not equal informed consent. Engineers and product managers share responsibility for foreseeable misuse, even when the misuse is performed by third parties.

Historical Background

Professional ethics in computing has roots in older engineering codes and medical ethics, but computing raised new questions because software is intangible, scalable, and often invisible. Key milestones include:

  • Early computer societies forming codes of conduct in the 1960s–70s.
  • ACM Code of Ethics revisions reflecting changing technology and social expectations.
  • IEEE Code of Ethics emphasizing public safety, welfare, and sustainable development.
  • Emergence of specialized guidance for AI, data science, and cybersecurity in the 2010s–2020s.

The field continues to evolve as generative AI, large-scale surveillance capabilities, and autonomous systems force new trade-offs into the open.

🧠

Core Concepts

Profession vs. Occupation

A profession is typically characterized by specialized knowledge, a commitment to serve clients and the public, self-regulation through codes and peer standards, and a sense of identity and responsibility that goes beyond employment contracts. Computing is still maturing as a profession, but the expectations placed on practitioners increasingly match those of traditional professions.

Ethics, Law, and Policy

Ethics concerns what ought to be done. Law defines what is required or prohibited by the state. Organizational policy sits between them. Something can be legal yet unethical, or ethical yet against company policy. Professionals must navigate all three layers.

Stakeholders

Every non-trivial computing decision affects multiple parties: end users, non-users who are profiled or affected, employers, shareholders, regulators, future maintainers, and broader society. Mapping stakeholders and their interests is a foundational professional skill.

Accountability and Transparency

Being able to explain why a system behaved as it did, who decided the objectives, and how trade-offs were resolved is increasingly required by regulation and by good engineering practice.

🔭

Multi-Perspective Analysis

⚖️ Ethical

What duties do we owe users and non-users? How do we balance autonomy, fairness, non-maleficence, and beneficence when they conflict?

📜 Legal

Which statutes, regulations, and liability doctrines apply? What documentation and process evidence would be needed if challenged?

💼 Business

How do ethical and legal risks translate into brand, regulatory, and operational risk? Where does short-term advantage create long-term liability?

🤖 AI / Data Science

How do model objectives, training data, evaluation metrics, and deployment context encode values? Who is accountable when the system errs?

🛠 Software Engineering

How do requirements, architecture, testing, and release processes embed or neglect professional obligations?

🔐 Cybersecurity

What are the duties of care around confidentiality, integrity, availability, and responsible disclosure?

📌

Concrete Examples

  • Hiring algorithms that amplify historical bias because the training data reflected past discrimination.
  • Content moderation systems that over- or under-enforce rules, affecting speech and safety differently across communities.
  • Medical decision support that works well on average but fails for underrepresented populations.
  • Open-source maintainers who receive little compensation yet are expected to patch critical vulnerabilities quickly.
  • Freelance developers asked to implement tracking or dark patterns without clear client disclosure.

Knowledge Check

1. Professional ethics in computing is best described as:
A set of personal moral beliefs that every engineer must share.
A disciplined practice of applying specialized knowledge with accountability to clients, users, and the public.
Whatever the employer’s code of conduct states.
Legal compliance only.
2. In the Therac-25 case, a central professional failure was:
Using an outdated programming language.
Inadequate safety design, poor response to operator reports, and over-reliance on software without sufficient hardware interlocks.
Insufficient marketing of the machine’s features.
Failure to open-source the control software.
3. When a product manager asks you to make a model "more aggressive" before a competitor launch, the professional response prioritizes:
Shipping as fast as possible to protect market share.
Clarifying impact on affected groups, documenting the change, ensuring review and monitoring, and escalating unresolved risks.
Implementing the change silently and monitoring later.
Refusing any model change under any circumstances.

Reflection Questions

1. Think of a computing system you use daily (social media feed, maps, banking app, search). Who are the stakeholders beyond the obvious user? What trade-offs might its designers have made?

2. Have you ever felt pressure (in a project, internship, or personal work) to cut corners on quality, testing, or honesty about limitations? How did you respond, and what would you do differently now?

3. In one sentence, how would you currently describe your professional identity as a computing student or emerging practitioner?

📋

Summary

  • Professionalism in computing combines technical competence with ethical judgment, accountability, and attention to societal impact.
  • Codes such as ACM and IEEE provide shared standards; they do not replace independent reasoning.
  • Historical failures (Therac-25, data-privacy scandals) show the human cost of treating software as "just code."
  • Every significant decision has ethical, legal, business, and technical dimensions; professionals learn to examine all of them.
  • Your habits of questioning, documenting, and escalating form the foundation of a trustworthy career.
⚡

Cheat Sheet

Ask before shipping: Who is affected? What could go wrong? How will we know? Who is accountable? Is the decision documented and reversible?

Three layers: Ethics (ought) · Law (must/must not) · Policy (organization’s rules).

Stakeholder map: Users · Non-users · Employer · Regulators · Maintainers · Society.

Codes: ACM Code of Ethics · IEEE Code of Ethics — read the principles, not only the slogans.
