Lecture 01 · Week 1

Introduction to Professional Practices in Computing

Why professional practices matter more than ever in the age of AI, data, and global software systems.

⏱ 45–60 min 📚 Reading: Baase Ch.1, Quinn Ch.1 🎯 6 Learning Objectives
🎯

Learning Objectives

  • Explain what “professional practices” means in computing disciplines (CS, SE, DS, AI, Cybersecurity).
  • Distinguish between legal compliance, ethical responsibility, and professional excellence.
  • Identify the major stakeholder groups affected by computing decisions.
  • Describe the role of professional codes (ACM, IEEE) at a high level.
  • Recognize why technical skill alone is insufficient for professional competence.
  • Reflect on personal values and how they will shape future professional identity.
📖

Introduction

Computing is no longer a purely technical craft practiced in isolation. Every line of code, every model trained, every system deployed, and every dataset collected has the potential to affect individuals, organizations, and societies at scale. Professional Practices in Computing is the discipline that prepares you to act responsibly within that reality.

This course is technology-independent. We will not teach you a particular programming language, framework, or cloud provider. Instead we focus on the reasoning, judgment, communication, and accountability skills that every computing professional needs—whether you become a software engineer, data scientist, AI researcher, security analyst, product manager, or entrepreneur.

Core Idea

Technical competence is necessary but not sufficient. Professional competence = technical skill + ethical reasoning + legal awareness + communication + lifelong learning + responsibility for impact.

🔥

Motivation: Why This Course Exists

In the last fifteen years we have witnessed repeated, high-profile failures that were not primarily caused by “bugs” in the classical sense, but by failures of professional judgment:

  • Social-media platforms optimizing for engagement while amplifying misinformation and polarization.
  • Machine-learning systems that encoded historical bias and produced discriminatory outcomes at scale.
  • Autonomous and safety-critical systems whose testing and verification processes were inadequate for the risk they posed.
  • Data breaches that exposed hundreds of millions of people because basic security and privacy practices were treated as optional.
  • Open-source maintainers burned out or projects abandoned because the social and economic model of contribution was unsustainable.

These are not edge cases. They are predictable outcomes when organizations and individuals treat computing as a purely technical activity rather than a socio-technical practice with real human stakes.

📰

Real Story: The Human Cost of “Just Shipping”

In 2018–2019 a major ride-sharing company faced intense public and regulatory scrutiny after a series of incidents involving its autonomous vehicle testing program and its treatment of drivers and data. Internal documents later revealed pressure to accelerate deployment timelines, under-investment in safety verification relative to the claimed autonomy level, and a culture that prioritized growth metrics over systematic risk analysis.

Engineers who raised concerns about edge cases or simulation coverage reported feeling that their voices were secondary to product and marketing schedules. The result was not only regulatory setbacks and reputational damage, but also a loss of public trust in the broader autonomous-driving industry that persists years later.

Professional lesson: When schedule and metrics dominate, the people who understand the residual risk are often the first to be sidelined. Professional practice requires creating (and protecting) channels for those concerns to be heard and acted upon.

Industrial Scenario

Scenario: You Join a Fast-Growing AI Startup

You are a new graduate hired as a machine-learning engineer at a 40-person startup building a hiring-recommendation system for mid-size companies. In your first month you discover that:

  • The training data is drawn primarily from the last five years of the founders’ previous employer’s hiring decisions.
  • There is no formal documentation of fairness metrics or demographic performance gaps.
  • The product is already being sold to customers with a “bias-free AI” marketing claim.
  • Your manager is under pressure from investors to hit a revenue target this quarter.

Discussion prompts:

  1. What are the professional obligations of an individual engineer in this situation?
  2. What organizational structures would make it easier (or harder) to raise the issue?
  3. How would you frame the conversation so that it is heard as risk management rather than obstruction?

Multi-Perspective View

Professional practice looks different depending on the lens you apply. Below are six complementary perspectives that will recur throughout the course.

⚖️ Ethical

What values are at stake? Who is harmed or benefited? Are we treating people as ends or merely as means?

📜 Legal

What statutes, regulations, and case law constrain or enable the action? What is the difference between legal and ethical?

💼 Business

What are the short- and long-term commercial consequences? Reputation, liability, talent retention, market access.

🤖 AI / Data

How do data provenance, model opacity, feedback loops, and scale change the nature of responsibility?

🛠️ Software Engineering

Requirements, verification, maintainability, technical debt, and the professional duty of care for systems that last.

🔒 Security

Threat models, least privilege, secure defaults, and the ethical obligation to protect users who cannot protect themselves.

⏳

Brief Historical Background

1940s–50s

Early computing as scientific instrument and military tool. Professional identity still forming.

1960s–70s

Software crisis. ACM and IEEE begin formalizing codes of ethics and educational recommendations.

1980s–90s

Personal computing and the internet. Privacy, intellectual property, and liability questions intensify.

2000s

Platform scale. Social networks, search, and cloud computing create new classes of societal impact.

2010s–present

AI, data science, and cyber-physical systems. Professional practice expands to include algorithmic accountability, sustainability, and global collaboration.

Key Concepts

ProfessionA vocation with specialized knowledge, a service orientation, and mechanisms of self-regulation and accountability.
ProfessionalSomeone who applies specialized knowledge for the benefit of clients/society and accepts responsibility for the consequences of that application.
Ethics vs LawLaw sets minimum enforceable standards. Ethics asks what we ought to do even when the law is silent or lagging.
StakeholderAnyone who can affect or is affected by a decision—users, non-users, employees, investors, communities, future generations.
AccountabilityThe obligation to explain and justify one’s decisions and to accept the consequences of those decisions.
Due care / Due diligenceThe standard of care a reasonable professional would exercise under similar circumstances.

Reflection

Personal prompt: Think of a computing product or system you use regularly that has caused (or could cause) harm to some group of people. What professional practices, if applied earlier, might have reduced that harm? Write 4–6 sentences.

📋

Summary

  • Professional practices in computing integrate technical excellence with ethical reasoning, legal awareness, communication, and accountability.
  • Computing decisions affect a wide range of stakeholders, many of whom have little power to influence the design process.
  • Legality is necessary but not sufficient; professionals are expected to exercise due care beyond minimum compliance.
  • Multiple perspectives (ethical, legal, business, AI/data, SE, security) help reveal blind spots.
  • Your professional identity begins forming now—through the habits of questioning, documenting, and speaking up.
