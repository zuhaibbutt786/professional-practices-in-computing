Lecture 01 — Introduction to Professional Practices in Computing

Why professional judgment matters as much as technical skill, and how this course prepares you for real responsibility.

Learning Objectives

  • Define professional practices in the context of computing disciplines.
  • Explain the difference between technical competence and professional responsibility.
  • Identify stakeholders affected by computing decisions (users, organizations, society, future generations).
  • Recognize that ethical, legal, business, and technical perspectives must be considered together.
  • Describe the structure and expectations of this course.

Motivation

Software, data systems, and AI now mediate credit decisions, medical triage, hiring, content visibility, infrastructure control, and national security. A technically correct system can still be harmful, unfair, illegal, or unsustainable. Professional practices train the judgment that sits above code: knowing when to stop, when to escalate, how to communicate risk, and how to act when incentives conflict with the public interest.

What “Professional” Means Here

A profession is not only a job. It involves specialized knowledge, a commitment to standards beyond the employer’s immediate demands, and accountability to a wider community. Computing has become a profession because its failures affect life, liberty, property, and dignity. Codes of ethics, licensing debates, and curriculum standards all respond to that reality.

This course is technology-independent. It teaches professional thinking rather than any specific tool or language.

Multi-Perspective View

Software Engineering

Requirements, testing, and release decisions carry professional weight. Shipping known defects without disclosure is a professional failure, not only a process failure.

AI & Data Science

Dataset choices, evaluation metrics, and deployment context determine whether a model helps or harms. “It worked on the test set” is never a complete professional answer.

Cybersecurity

Access, disclosure, and dual-use knowledge require clear authorization and responsibility. Competence without ethics becomes a threat.

Business & Legal

Contracts, liability, regulation, and reputation constrain what is permissible. Professionals must understand enough law and organizational reality to act wisely.

Opening Case — Why This Course Exists

Across decades, high-profile failures (unsafe medical devices, biased decision systems, large-scale privacy breaches, infrastructure outages caused by configuration and process gaps) have shared a pattern: technical teams optimized for a narrow goal while underestimating broader harm, communication failure, or accountability gaps. Professional practices aim to make those wider considerations routine rather than exceptional.

Summary

Professional practices prepare computing graduates to exercise judgment under uncertainty, conflict of interest, and social impact. Technical skill is necessary but not sufficient. This course develops the habits of multi-perspective analysis, ethical reasoning, and accountable communication required by modern professional standards.

Cheat Sheet

  • Technical correctness ≠ professional adequacy
  • Stakeholders extend beyond the immediate user and employer
  • Multiple lenses (ethical, legal, business, SE, AI, security) must be applied together
  • Codes and standards exist to support judgment, not replace it

Glossary

Professional practices
The norms, judgment, and accountability expected of computing practitioners beyond pure technical skill.
Stakeholder
Any party affected by a system or decision, including users, non-users, organizations, regulators, and society.
Public interest
The welfare of society as a whole, often prioritized in professional codes over narrow private gain.
