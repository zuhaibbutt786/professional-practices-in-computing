Module 01 ~45–60 min

Introduction to Professional Practices in Computing

Why technical excellence alone is not enough — and how professional judgment, ethical reasoning, and multi-perspective analysis become core competencies for every computing graduate.

Learning Objectives

  • Articulate why professional practices matter beyond writing correct code
  • Describe the dual role of the computing professional as Creator and Steward
  • Identify key dimensions of professional competence (ethical, legal, social, organizational)
  • Explain how historical failures illuminate the need for professional judgment
  • Apply a multi-perspective lens (ethical, legal, business, AI, data, SE, security) to simple scenarios

1. Motivation: Why This Course Exists

Computing systems now mediate health care, finance, justice, education, transportation, and public discourse. A single design decision can affect millions of people. Technical skill lets you build systems that work; professional skill helps you decide whether they should work that way, for whom, under what conditions, and with what safeguards.

This course is deliberately technology-independent. It does not teach a programming language or a framework. It teaches professional thinking: how to reason about responsibility, impact, trade-offs, and the long-term consequences of technical choices.

2. Real Story: When Code Meets Consequence

Consider the Therac-25 radiation therapy accidents (1985–1987). A combination of software race conditions, removal of hardware interlocks, inadequate testing, and weak organizational response led to massive overdoses. Patients were harmed or killed. The engineers who wrote the software were competent in their domain; the failure was systemic — process, verification, culture, and the assumption that software alone could replace proven safety mechanisms.

Similar patterns appear in more recent incidents: autonomous vehicle crashes, biased hiring algorithms, large-scale data breaches, and content-moderation failures. In each case, the technical artifact was only part of the story. Professional judgment about risk, evidence, stakeholder impact, and escalation was equally decisive.

3. Industrial Scenario

You join a product team shipping a recommendation system used by tens of millions of users. The model optimizes engagement. Engagement metrics improve. Leadership is pleased. A junior engineer notices that certain demographic groups receive systematically lower-quality recommendations and that some content clusters appear to reinforce harmful stereotypes. Raising the issue requires navigating product incentives, incomplete data about harm, and the risk of being labeled "not a team player."

This is not a pure coding problem. It is a professional-practice problem: how to surface concerns, gather evidence, propose mitigations, and escalate when necessary — while remaining constructive and grounded in both technical and ethical reasoning.

4. The Dual Role: Creator and Steward

Every computing professional occupies two roles simultaneously:

  • Creator — designs, builds, tests, and ships systems.
  • Steward — considers impact, anticipates misuse, protects users and the public, and maintains the integrity of the profession.

Neither role is optional. Excellence in creation without stewardship produces brittle, harmful, or untrustworthy systems. Stewardship without creation produces only commentary. The course trains both.

5. Multi-Perspective Analysis

Professional practice requires examining decisions through multiple lenses:

  • Ethical — duties, rights, harms, fairness, autonomy
  • Legal — statutes, liability, regulation, contracts
  • Business — incentives, reputation, risk, sustainability
  • AI / Data Science — bias, opacity, data provenance, model risk
  • Software Engineering — quality, maintainability, verification, technical debt
  • Cybersecurity — threats, privacy, resilience, disclosure

No single perspective is sufficient. The course repeatedly practices moving between them.

6. Professional Identity and Career

Professional identity is not a job title. It is a commitment to competence, honesty, and the public interest. ACM and IEEE codes of ethics articulate this commitment. Employers increasingly expect graduates who can discuss ethics, privacy, accessibility, and responsible AI in interviews and on the job.

This course also covers communication, teamwork, leadership, conflict resolution, continuous learning, and career development — the practical skills that turn technical ability into professional impact.

7. Course Roadmap

Subsequent modules deepen the foundations introduced here: history of computing ethics and professional codes, ethical theories and decision frameworks, ACM and IEEE codes in detail, professional responsibility and accountability, privacy and data protection, AI ethics and responsible AI, intellectual property and open source, communication and teamwork, and emerging challenges in a digital society.

Knowledge Check

Reflection Questions

  1. Describe a computing system you use daily. Who are the stakeholders who might be helped or harmed by design decisions in that system?
  2. When have you observed (or imagined) a tension between "shipping fast" and "shipping responsibly"? How would you surface that tension constructively?
  3. Which of the multi-perspective lenses feels most natural to you right now? Which feels least familiar? Why?

Summary

  • Technical competence and professional judgment are complementary.
  • Computing professionals act as both Creators and Stewards.
  • Historical failures (e.g., Therac-25) show that culture, process, and verification matter as much as individual coding skill.
  • Multi-perspective analysis (ethical, legal, business, AI, data, SE, security) is a core professional habit.
  • This course builds the professional thinking required by ACM/IEEE guidelines and by contemporary industry practice.
