Lecture 01 — Introduction & Professionalism

Why professionalism matters in computing, what a professional identity looks like, and how this course prepares you for ethical and responsible practice.

Learning Objectives

  • Define professional practice in computing beyond technical skill
  • Explain the roles of ACM and IEEE codes of ethics
  • Identify stakeholders affected by computing decisions
  • Apply multi-perspective analysis (ethical, legal, business, AI, data, SE, security)
  • Reflect on personal professional identity and lifelong learning

Introduction & Motivation

Computing professionals design systems that shape how people work, learn, vote, receive healthcare, and relate to one another. Technical competence is necessary but not sufficient. Professionalism includes judgment under uncertainty, accountability for outcomes, clear communication, and respect for human values.

This course treats professional practice as a discipline of thinking: ethical reasoning, risk awareness, legal literacy, and organisational awareness — independent of any particular programming language or framework.

Real Story

When software engineers at a major social platform optimised for engagement metrics without sufficient regard for polarisation and mental health, the resulting systems amplified harmful content at global scale. The technical work succeeded; the professional judgment about societal impact lagged. Cases like this illustrate why multi-stakeholder and multi-perspective analysis is a core professional skill.

Industrial Scenario

You join a startup building a recommendation system for a news app. Product leadership wants maximisation of time-on-site. You notice the model rewards emotionally charged content. What do you do? How do you frame the issue to product, legal, and leadership? This course gives you language and frameworks for that conversation.

Case Study Snapshot

Background: Large platforms and AI systems have repeatedly demonstrated that optimisation for narrow metrics can produce wide negative externalities.

Ethical issue: Duty of care to users and society versus commercial incentives.

Professional lesson: Professionals must surface risks early, document concerns, and escalate when necessary.

Concept Explanation

Professionalism in computing combines:

  • Technical mastery and continuous learning
  • Ethical sensitivity and structured decision-making
  • Legal awareness (privacy, IP, safety, discrimination)
  • Communication and teamwork across disciplines
  • Accountability for the systems you help create

ACM and IEEE codes provide shared language. They are not checklists; they are starting points for deliberation.

Multi-Perspective View

Ethical: Harm prevention, fairness, autonomy, transparency.

Legal: Compliance is necessary but often insufficient; law lags technology.

Business: Long-term trust and brand risk versus short-term metrics.

AI / Data: Bias, feedback loops, data provenance, model accountability.

Software Engineering: Requirements that include safety and ethics; design for failure modes.

Cyber Security: Security as a professional obligation to users and organisations.

Reflection Questions

  1. What does “being a professional” mean to you beyond getting a job?
  2. Describe a time you saw (or can imagine) a technical decision that was correct in code but questionable in impact.
  3. Which stakeholder groups do you tend to under-weight when designing systems?

Knowledge Check

Summary & Cheat Sheet

  • Professionalism = skill + judgment + accountability
  • Use multi-perspective analysis on every significant decision
  • Codes of ethics are tools for deliberation, not rigid rules
  • Document concerns; escalate when risk is material
  • Lifelong learning is part of the professional obligation

Further Reading

  • ACM Code of Ethics and Professional Conduct
  • IEEE Code of Ethics
  • Baase, A Gift of Fire (selected chapters)
  • Quinn, Ethics for the Information Age
