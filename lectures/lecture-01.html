Lecture 01 · Week 1

Introduction to Professional Practices in Computing

Why professionalism matters, how computing became a profession, and the roadmap for ethical and responsible practice across CS, SE, AI, Data Science, and Cybersecurity.

⏱ 40–55 min 📚 Reading: Baase Ch.1, Quinn Ch.1, ACM Code preamble 🎯 6 Learning Objectives
🎯

Learning Objectives

  • Define professional practices in computing and distinguish them from purely technical skill.
  • Explain why ACM and IEEE treat ethical and professional competence as core graduate outcomes.
  • Identify the multi-perspective nature of modern computing dilemmas (ethical, legal, business, technical, societal).
  • Describe the structure, expectations, and assessment philosophy of this course.
  • Articulate a provisional personal professional identity as a future computing practitioner.
  • Apply a simple decision checklist to a realistic industrial scenario.
🔥

Motivation

Computing systems now mediate health, finance, education, democracy, infrastructure, and interpersonal relationships. A single design choice—how a ranking algorithm treats engagement, how a model handles under-represented groups, how an access-control list is reviewed—can amplify or mitigate harm at scale. Technical excellence alone is insufficient. Professional practices supply the judgment, communication, and accountability frameworks that turn technical skill into responsible impact.

Core Idea

Professionalism is the disciplined habit of asking not only “Can we build it?” but “Should we build it this way, for these people, under these constraints, with these residual risks?”

📰

Real Story: Therac-25 and the Cost of Incomplete Professional Norms

Between 1985 and 1987 the Therac-25 radiation therapy machine delivered massive overdoses to several patients. Investigation revealed software race conditions, inadequate hardware interlocks, poor user-interface design, and a culture that treated software as secondary to mechanical engineering. Operators and engineers lacked clear professional channels and shared standards for raising safety concerns about software behavior.

The case became a canonical teaching example in software engineering ethics. It demonstrated that technical competence without systematic attention to safety, verification, and communication of residual risk produces preventable harm. Modern codes explicitly elevate public safety, thorough evaluation, and honest communication of limitations—principles that were still consolidating when Therac-25 was fielded.

Professional lesson: Historical failures are not ancient history; they are the reason contemporary codes contain the clauses they do. When you invoke “avoid harm” or “strive for high quality,” you are standing on the shoulders of those investigations.

Industrial Scenario

Scenario: Shipping a Generative Coding Assistant

You are a junior engineer at a startup building a generative AI coding assistant. Product wants to ship a feature that automatically includes code snippets from public GitHub repositories in generated answers. Legal has not yet signed off on license compliance. Marketing has already announced the feature for next week’s launch. Your manager asks you to “keep the feature behind a flag and ship the rest; we can sort licenses later.”

Discussion prompts:

  1. What professional obligations (competence, honesty, public interest, intellectual property) are in tension?
  2. What documentation and escalation steps would a professional take before agreeing or refusing?
  3. How would you frame the residual risk to non-technical stakeholders?
🔭

Multi-Perspective View

🛠️ Software Engineering

Professional practice demands process discipline, truthful status reporting, independent review, and refusing to release systems whose residual risk has not been adequately characterized.

🤖 AI & Data Science

Modern interpretations extend classic principles to dataset bias, model opacity, feedback loops, and the duty to document limitations of predictive systems.

🔒 Cybersecurity

Responsible disclosure, authorization boundaries, and dual-use awareness appear as applications of honesty, public interest, and “access only when authorized.”

💼 Business & Legal

Codes support duty-of-care arguments and influence how organizations structure escalation, post-incident review, and external communication of risk.

💡

Key Concepts

Professional practicesThe disciplined habits, standards, and accountability frameworks that turn technical skill into responsible societal impact.
Professional identityThe internalized sense of belonging to a community that shares values, knowledge, and obligations beyond any single employer.
Multi-perspective reasoningExamining a dilemma from ethical, legal, business, technical, and societal viewpoints before deciding.
Residual riskThe risk that remains after mitigation; professionals must characterize and communicate it honestly.
Duty of careThe obligation to act as a reasonable professional would under similar circumstances, informed by codes and historical lessons.

Reflection

Personal prompt: Think of a recent technology news story. Identify at least one ethical, one legal, and one business dimension of the issue. How might a computing professional have influenced the outcome? Write 5–8 sentences.

📋

Summary

  • Professional practices extend technical skill with judgment, communication, and accountability.
  • Historical failures (e.g., Therac-25) directly shaped modern codes and teaching cases.
  • Modern dilemmas require multi-perspective analysis: ethical, legal, business, technical, societal.
  • This course builds habits of reasoning that transfer across CS, SE, AI, Data Science, and Cybersecurity.
  • Codes and frameworks are decision aids and shared language, not mere compliance checklists.
