Module 02

History of the Computing Profession & Codes of Ethics

Professionalization · Software crisis · Early codes · ACM/IEEE milestones

Learning Objectives

  • Trace key stages in the professionalization of computing and software engineering
  • Explain why the software crisis of the 1960s–70s drove demand for professional standards
  • Summarize the evolution of ACM and IEEE codes of ethics at a high level
  • Identify recurring patterns in how the profession responds to new technological risks
  • Articulate how historical failures inform contemporary professional responsibility

1. Motivation — Why History Matters

Professional codes are not abstract philosophy. They are responses to real harms, public distrust, and the recognition that specialized knowledge creates obligations. Understanding how the field arrived at current norms helps practitioners interpret and apply those norms when technology changes again.

2. Real Story — The Software Crisis

NATO Software Engineering Conferences (1968–1969)

Projects overran budgets, missed deadlines, and delivered unreliable systems. The term "software engineering" was deliberately chosen to signal that software development needed the discipline of engineering: requirements, design, verification, and professional standards—not only clever coding.

Lesson: Professional identity and technical method evolved together under pressure from failure.

3. Historical Timeline (Selected Milestones)

  • 1940s–50s — Computing as research and specialized industrial practice; limited public impact awareness
  • 1960s–70s — Software crisis; birth of software engineering as a named discipline
  • 1970s–80s — Early professional codes; ACM and IEEE activity; growth of CS education
  • 1990s–2000s — Internet scale, Y2K, security and privacy enter mainstream professional concern
  • 2010s–2020s — Big data, AI, platform power; major revisions (e.g., ACM 2018); regulatory interest (GDPR, AI Act concepts)

4. Multi-Perspective View

Software Engineering

From ad-hoc programming to lifecycle models, process standards, and professional licensure debates—history shows repeated attempts to make quality and safety systematic.

AI & Data

Earlier codes assumed relatively static systems. Modern AI introduces learning after deployment, opacity, and scale of impact that earlier frameworks only partially anticipated.

Security

Security moved from physical access control to network, application, and supply-chain concerns. Professional norms around disclosure and dual-use research evolved with each wave of incidents.

Business & Legal

Liability, consumer protection, and sector regulation increasingly treat software as a product and a service with duties of care—not merely a contractual deliverable.

Summary

  • Professionalization of computing was driven by scale, failure, and public impact—not only by technical progress.
  • Codes of ethics evolved as the profession recognized duties beyond the immediate employer or client.
  • History provides patterns: underestimation of risk, delayed response to new harms, and gradual institutionalization of standards.
