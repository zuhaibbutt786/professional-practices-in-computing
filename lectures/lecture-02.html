Lecture 02 · Week 2

History of the Profession & Codes of Ethics

How computing became a profession, why codes of ethics emerged, and what they demand of practitioners today.

⏱ 45–60 min 📚 Reading: Baase Ch.1–2, Quinn Ch.1–2, ACM Code 🎯 5 Learning Objectives
🎯

Learning Objectives

  • Trace key milestones in the professionalization of computing from the 1940s to the present.
  • Explain why formal codes of ethics became necessary as systems moved into critical domains.
  • Compare the roles of ACM, IEEE Computer Society, and related bodies in shaping professional norms.
  • Identify recurring themes across historical and modern codes (public interest, competence, honesty, fairness).
  • Relate historical failures (Therac-25, Ariane 5, privacy scandals) to the content of modern codes.
🔥

Motivation

Codes of ethics are not decorative statements. They crystallize lessons from decades of accidents, scandals, and near-misses. Understanding how and why they were written helps practitioners apply them under real pressure instead of treating them as compliance checklists.

Core Idea

A code is a living summary of hard-won professional judgment. It is useful only when engineers, data scientists, and security practitioners can recall its principles under deadline pressure and use them as shared language with managers and clients.

⏳

Historical Arc of Professionalization

1940s–1950s

Computing as scientific instrument and military tool. Professional identity still forming; early ACM (1947) and IEEE Computer Society roots.

1960s–1970s

Software crisis. Cost and reliability failures in large systems. ACM and IEEE begin formalizing codes of ethics and educational recommendations.

1980s–1990s

Personal computing and the internet. Privacy, intellectual property, liability, and safety-critical systems (Therac-25, Ariane 5) intensify the need for shared standards.

2000s

Platform scale. Social networks, search, and cloud computing create new classes of societal impact and new interpretations of classic principles.

2010s–present

AI, data science, and cyber-physical systems. Professional practice expands to algorithmic accountability, sustainability, dual-use research, and global collaboration. Codes are updated (ACM 2018) and interpreted for new domains.

📰

Real Story: Therac-25 and the Cost of Incomplete Professional Norms

Between 1985 and 1987 the Therac-25 radiation therapy machine delivered massive overdoses to several patients. Investigation revealed software race conditions, inadequate hardware interlocks, poor user-interface design, and a culture that treated software as secondary to mechanical engineering. Operators and engineers lacked clear professional channels and shared standards for raising safety concerns about software behavior.

The case became a canonical teaching example in software engineering ethics. It demonstrated that technical competence without systematic attention to safety, verification, and communication of residual risk produces preventable harm. Modern codes explicitly elevate public safety, thorough evaluation, and honest communication of limitations—principles that were still consolidating when Therac-25 was fielded.

Professional lesson: Historical failures are not ancient history; they are the reason contemporary codes contain the clauses they do. When you invoke “avoid harm” or “strive for high quality,” you are standing on the shoulders of those investigations.

Industrial Scenario

Scenario: Pressure to Ship Before Code Review Completes

You are a mid-level engineer on a team building a medical-device companion app. A major hospital customer has a go-live date in three weeks. The remaining open issues include incomplete hazard analysis documentation and two unresolved edge cases in the dose-calculation path. Your manager asks the team to “document the residual risk and ship; we can patch later.”

Discussion prompts:

  1. Which historical lessons (Therac-25 or others) are relevant?
  2. Which principles from ACM or IEEE codes give you language to push back constructively?
  3. What documentation and escalation steps would a professional take?

Multi-Perspective View

🛠️ Software Engineering

Codes reinforce process discipline, truthful status reporting, independent review, and refusing to release systems whose residual risk has not been adequately characterized.

🤖 AI & Data Science

Modern interpretations extend classic principles to dataset bias, model opacity, feedback loops, and the duty to document limitations of predictive systems.

🔒 Cybersecurity

Responsible disclosure, authorization boundaries, and dual-use awareness appear as applications of honesty, public interest, and “access only when authorized.”

💼 Business & Legal

Codes support duty-of-care arguments and influence how organizations structure escalation, post-incident review, and external communication of risk.

Key Concepts

ProfessionalizationThe process by which an occupation develops specialized knowledge, education standards, codes, and mechanisms of self-regulation.
Code of ethicsA formal statement of values and expected conduct for members of a profession; a living document refined by experience.
Public interestThe welfare of society as a whole, prioritized over narrow private or organizational gain in professional codes.
CompetenceThe obligation to maintain and apply the knowledge and skill required for the work one undertakes, and to acknowledge limits.
Due careThe standard of care a reasonable professional would exercise under similar circumstances, informed by codes and historical lessons.

Summary

  • The computing profession developed codes of ethics in response to growing societal impact and repeated failures.
  • Public interest, competence, honesty, and fairness are recurring themes.
  • Historical cases such as Therac-25 directly informed modern emphasis on safety, verification, and communication of residual risk.
  • Codes remain living documents that guide judgment, training, and accountability across SE, AI, data, and security practice.
  • Use codes as decision aids and shared language, not as checklists to be filed away.
