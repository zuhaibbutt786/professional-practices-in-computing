Module 02 · Ethics Core

Ethics and Professional Responsibility

Ethical theories applied to computing, practical decision frameworks, the meaning of professional responsibility, and how to act when organizational incentives conflict with public interest.

⏱ ~90–120 min📚 Theories + frameworks + cases🎯 Ethics
🎯

Learning Objectives

  • Apply major ethical theories (utilitarianism, deontology, virtue ethics, care ethics) to computing dilemmas.
  • Use a structured ethical decision-making process on realistic scenarios.
  • Interpret key principles from ACM and IEEE codes and map them to daily engineering decisions.
  • Distinguish individual, organizational, and systemic responsibility.
  • Evaluate when and how whistleblowing or escalation is professionally justified.
  • Analyze power, incentives, and culture as drivers of ethical or unethical outcomes.
📖

Introduction

Ethics is not a list of prohibitions. It is the practice of reasoning about what we ought to do when values conflict, when consequences are uncertain, and when multiple stakeholders have legitimate claims. In computing, those conflicts appear in product priorities, data use, security trade-offs, AI objectives, and the treatment of users and non-users.

This module gives you theories and a reusable process so that ethical reasoning becomes a professional habit rather than an after-the-fact rationalization.

🧭

Ethical Theories in Brief

Utilitarianism

Focus on consequences: maximize overall well-being or minimize harm. Useful for risk-benefit analysis; weak when it ignores rights of minorities or hard-to-measure harms.

Deontology

Focus on duties and rules: some actions are required or forbidden regardless of outcome (honesty, respect for autonomy, non-maleficence). Strong on rights; can be rigid when duties conflict.

Virtue Ethics

Focus on character: what would a wise, just, courageous professional do? Emphasizes habits, judgment, and role models over pure rules or calculations.

Care / Relational Ethics

Focus on relationships, vulnerability, and context. Especially relevant for systems that affect dependent users, patients, or marginalized communities.

Practical tip

Professionals rarely apply one theory in isolation. Use them as lenses: ask “What are the consequences?”, “What duties apply?”, “What character does this decision reflect?”, and “Who is vulnerable and how are relationships affected?”

🧩

A Practical Decision Framework

  1. Identify the decision and the facts you know and do not know.
  2. Map stakeholders and their interests (including those who cannot speak for themselves).
  3. List options, including “do nothing” and “escalate / delay”.
  4. Apply ethical lenses (consequences, duties, virtues, care).
  5. Check codes, law, and policy for constraints and guidance.
  6. Choose, document rationale, and define monitoring for unexpected outcomes.
  7. Review after the fact and update practices.
📜

Professional Codes in Practice

ACM and IEEE codes are not checklists for compliance theater. They articulate shared expectations: contribute to society and human well-being; avoid harm; be honest and trustworthy; be fair and take action not to discriminate; respect privacy; honor confidentiality; maintain competence; know and respect existing laws; accept and provide appropriate professional review; give comprehensive evaluations; improve public understanding; access computing resources only when authorized.

When a code principle and an organizational incentive conflict, the professional’s obligation is to the higher standard of the profession and the public interest, not merely to the employer’s short-term preference.

🏛

Individual, Organizational, and Systemic Responsibility

Blame is rarely located only in one person. Systems produce outcomes through incentives, culture, process design, and individual choices. Professionals still retain agency: they can ask questions, document concerns, propose safer alternatives, and, in extreme cases, refuse or report. Organizations have duties to create cultures where speaking up is safe and rewarded. Regulators and society set boundaries when self-regulation fails.

🔍

Case Focus: Boeing 737 MAX

Design decisions around the MCAS system, training assumptions, certification processes, and internal communication contributed to two fatal crashes. Investigations highlighted pressure to minimize training differences, inadequate disclosure, and cultural factors that discouraged dissent. The case illustrates how technical, organizational, and ethical failures intertwine — and why professional responsibility cannot stop at “I only implemented the requirement.”

✅

Knowledge Check

1. A key strength of applying multiple ethical theories is:
Guaranteeing a single correct answer every time.
Revealing different aspects of a dilemma (consequences, duties, character, relationships) that one lens might miss.
Eliminating the need for professional codes.
Proving that ethics is purely subjective.
2. Professional responsibility is best understood as:
Following every instruction from a manager without question.
Exercising judgment, competence, and accountability toward clients, users, and the public within the standards of the profession.
Avoiding any personal risk by never raising concerns.
Maximizing shareholder value above all other considerations.
🪞

Reflection

Describe a time (real or hypothetical) when following orders conflicted with what you believed was right. What options did you have, and what would a professional response look like?

📋

Summary

  • Ethical theories provide complementary lenses; use them together.
  • A structured process (facts → stakeholders → options → lenses → codes/law → decide & monitor) improves judgment.
  • Codes express the profession’s shared standards; they guide when incentives pull elsewhere.
  • Responsibility is distributed, but individuals retain agency and duties.
