Lecture 02 · Ethics

Ethics Frameworks & Decision Making

Utilitarianism, deontology, virtue ethics, and structured processes for technology dilemmas.

Learning Objectives

  • Apply utilitarianism, deontology, and virtue ethics to computing cases.
  • Use a structured ethical decision process under uncertainty.
  • Distinguish legal compliance from ethical responsibility.
  • Recognize common cognitive biases that distort ethical judgment in tech teams.

Motivation

Engineers and data scientists routinely face trade-offs: speed vs. safety, engagement vs. well-being, accuracy vs. fairness. Without shared language for ethical reasoning, teams default to product pressure or personal intuition. Frameworks make reasoning explicit, contestable, and improvable.

Three Classical Lenses

Utilitarianism evaluates actions by consequences—typically overall welfare or reduction of harm. Useful for risk analysis and prioritization; weak when rights of minorities are sacrificed for majority benefit.

Deontology focuses on duties and rules (honesty, informed consent, non-maleficence). Useful for hard boundaries; can be rigid when duties conflict.

Virtue ethics asks what a good professional would do—cultivating honesty, courage, humility, and care. Useful for culture and character; less precise for one-off decisions.

Practice tip: Run the same case through all three lenses. Where they agree, confidence rises. Where they diverge, you have identified the real tension.

Industrial Scenario

A ranking team can boost short-term revenue by promoting high-engagement content that correlates with polarization. Legal review finds no clear violation. Product leadership wants the change. How do you structure the ethical analysis before deciding whether to escalate?

Decision Process (practical)

  1. Clarify facts and uncertainty.
  2. Identify stakeholders and foreseeable harms/benefits.
  3. Map applicable duties, policies, and laws.
  4. Generate options (including "do nothing" and "delay").
  5. Evaluate options under multiple ethical lenses.
  6. Choose, document rationale, and plan monitoring.

Knowledge Check

Reflection

Reflection

Describe a past decision (work, study, or personal project) where ethical tension was present. Which framework would have clarified the trade-off?

Assignment

Choose one real public tech incident. Analyze it with utilitarianism, deontology, and virtue ethics (300–500 words). Conclude with one recommendation a professional on the team could have made earlier.

Summary

Ethical frameworks do not remove hard choices; they make reasoning transparent. Structured decision processes turn moral intuition into professional practice that can be reviewed and improved.
