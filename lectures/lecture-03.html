Module 03 · Data & Society

Privacy, Data Protection & Surveillance

Why privacy matters, core data-protection principles, consent and its limits, surveillance risks, and the professional duties of anyone who designs systems that collect or process personal data.

⏱ ~90–120 min📚 Principles + cases🎯 Privacy
🎯

Learning Objectives

  • Explain privacy as a multi-dimensional concept (informational, decisional, spatial, associational).
  • Apply data-protection principles: lawfulness, purpose limitation, data minimization, accuracy, storage limitation, integrity, accountability.
  • Critique consent as a primary mechanism and identify when it is insufficient.
  • Analyze surveillance and profiling risks in commercial and governmental contexts.
  • Map professional responsibilities for engineers and data scientists handling personal data.
💡

Why Privacy Matters

Privacy is not secrecy. It is the ability to control boundaries around personal information and to participate in society without constant observation or unwanted interference. Computing systems expand the scale, speed, and opacity of data collection, making privacy a first-order design concern rather than an afterthought.

📐

Core Data-Protection Principles

  • Lawfulness, fairness, transparency — process data on a valid basis and be clear with people.
  • Purpose limitation — collect for specified purposes; do not repurpose without justification.
  • Data minimization — collect only what is needed.
  • Accuracy — keep data correct and up to date where relevant.
  • Storage limitation — retain only as long as necessary.
  • Integrity and confidentiality — protect against unauthorized access and loss.
  • Accountability — be able to demonstrate compliance and responsible practice.
Note on regulation

Frameworks such as the EU GDPR, and similar laws elsewhere, operationalize these principles. Professionals should understand the spirit of the principles even when working under different legal regimes.

✋

Consent and Its Limits

Click-through consent is often neither informed nor free. Dark patterns, length of policies, bundling of purposes, and power imbalances undermine meaningful choice. Professionals should prefer data minimization and purpose limitation over relying on weak consent, and should design for genuine control where consent is required.

🔍

Cases

Equifax (2017) — Massive breach of sensitive personal and financial data; failures in patching, monitoring, and response highlighted duties of care around security of personal data.

Cambridge Analytica / Facebook — Secondary use and third-party access of social data for political profiling; consent and platform design failures.

Clearview AI and facial recognition — Scraping public photos to build identification databases raises questions about expectation of privacy in public spaces and secondary use of publicly available data.

🔭

Multi-Perspective

Ethical

Respect for persons, autonomy, non-maleficence, justice in who bears surveillance burdens.

Legal

Data-protection statutes, sectoral laws, liability for breaches, cross-border transfers.

Business

Trust as asset; cost of breaches and regulation; competitive advantage of privacy-respecting design.

Technical

Privacy by design, differential privacy, access controls, minimization in architecture and logs.

✅

Knowledge Check

Data minimization means:
Collecting as much data as possible for future unknown uses.
Collecting only the personal data that is adequate, relevant, and limited to what is necessary for the specified purposes.
Deleting all data after 30 days regardless of purpose.
Encrypting every field in a database.
📋

Summary

  • Privacy is foundational to autonomy and trust in digital systems.
  • Data-protection principles guide design beyond mere legal compliance.
  • Consent alone is often insufficient; minimize and purpose-limit by default.
  • Professionals share responsibility for foreseeable privacy harms.
