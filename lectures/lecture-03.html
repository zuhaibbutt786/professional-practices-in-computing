Module 03

Ethical Theories & Decision Frameworks

Utilitarianism · Deontology · Virtue ethics · Practical decision procedures

Learning Objectives

  • Summarize major ethical theories used in professional computing discussions
  • Apply utilitarian, deontological, and virtue-ethics lenses to the same dilemma
  • Use a structured decision procedure under uncertainty and conflicting duties
  • Recognize limits of pure theory when incentives, culture, and incomplete information dominate
  • Connect ethical reasoning to documentation, stakeholder analysis, and professional codes

1. Motivation

Professionals face trade-offs: privacy vs. security, speed vs. safety, individual fairness vs. group benefit. Ethical theories do not hand down unique answers in every case, but they supply vocabulary and structure so that decisions can be explained, challenged, and improved rather than rationalized after the fact.

2. Core Theories (Practical Overview)

Utilitarianism / Consequentialism

Focus on outcomes: maximize overall good (or minimize harm). Strength: forces explicit consideration of impacts. Weakness: measurement problems, minority rights, and long-term uncertainty.

Deontology (Duty-based)

Focus on rules and rights: some actions are required or forbidden regardless of aggregate benefit. Strength: protects individuals and process integrity. Weakness: rule conflicts and rigidity in novel situations.

Virtue Ethics

Focus on character: what would a responsible, honest, careful professional do? Strength: emphasizes habits and culture. Weakness: less precise guidance for one-off dilemmas.

Care / Relational Ethics

Focus on relationships, vulnerability, and context. Useful in healthcare, education, and community-facing systems where pure aggregation misses important harms.

3. A Practical Decision Procedure

  1. Identify stakeholders and potential harms/benefits (including second-order effects)
  2. List applicable duties, rights, laws, and organizational policies
  3. Generate feasible options (including "do nothing" and escalation)
  4. Evaluate options under more than one ethical lens
  5. Choose, document rationale, and define monitoring/review triggers

Documentation matters: professional accountability requires that reasoning can be inspected later.

4. Industrial Scenario

Feature flag for sensitive data processing

A product team can enable a high-value analytics feature by processing user content that may include personal data. Marketing wants it on by default; legal wants explicit consent; engineering can ship either way in two weeks. Apply the decision procedure above: who is affected, which duties apply, what options exist, and how would different ethical theories rank the options?

Reflection

  1. Take a recent technology controversy you know. Analyze it once under a consequentialist lens and once under a rights/duties lens. Where do the recommendations diverge?
  2. Which part of a structured decision procedure do teams most often skip under deadline pressure? What is the cost of skipping it?

Summary

  • Ethical theories provide complementary lenses, not a single algorithm for every case.
  • Structured procedures and documentation turn private intuition into professional practice.
  • Culture and incentives determine whether good reasoning is possible under pressure.
