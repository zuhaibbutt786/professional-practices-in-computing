Ethical Theories & Decision Frameworks

Core ethical theories used in professional reasoning — and practical frameworks for applying them to computing dilemmas without reducing ethics to slogans.

Learning Objectives

  • Explain utilitarianism, deontology, virtue ethics, and care ethics in plain language
  • Apply each theory to a computing scenario and note strengths and limitations
  • Use a structured decision framework (identify stakeholders, options, consequences, duties, character)
  • Recognize when theories conflict and how professionals justify choices
  • Connect theory to multi-perspective analysis used throughout the course

1. Why Theory Matters

Ethical theories are not academic ornaments. They supply vocabulary and tests for arguments about harm, rights, fairness, and character. When teams disagree about the right action, theory helps surface the underlying values rather than remaining stuck in intuition or authority.

2. Utilitarianism (Consequentialism)

Focuses on outcomes: the right action tends to maximize overall good (or minimize harm) for those affected. Strengths: forces quantification of impact and comparison of options. Limitations: measurement problems, minority sacrifice, and uncertainty about long-term consequences — all acute in AI and large-scale systems.

3. Deontology (Duty-Based Ethics)

Focuses on rules, rights, and duties independent of outcomes. Certain actions are wrong even if they produce good results (e.g., deception, violation of autonomy). Strengths: protects individuals against being used as mere means. Limitations: rigid rules can conflict; hard cases require judgment about which duties take priority.

4. Virtue Ethics

Asks what a person of good character would do and what habits of excellence the profession should cultivate (honesty, courage, justice, humility, care). Strengths: emphasizes long-term professional identity and culture. Limitations: less precise for one-off decisions under time pressure.

5. Care Ethics & Relational Approaches

Emphasizes relationships, vulnerability, and responsibilities of care. Particularly relevant to systems that affect dependents, patients, children, or marginalized groups. Complements rights-based and outcome-based thinking.

6. Practical Decision Framework

  1. Clarify the decision and constraints
  2. Identify stakeholders and possible harms/benefits
  3. List options (including "do nothing" and escalation)
  4. Evaluate options through consequential, duty, and character lenses
  5. Choose, document reasoning, and plan monitoring

This structure is reusable in design reviews, incident response, and policy discussions.

Knowledge Check

Reflection Questions

  1. Take a recent tech controversy. How would a utilitarian analysis differ from a deontological one?
  2. Which virtues do you think computing professionals most need to cultivate in the age of AI?
  3. When have you seen outcome-focused and duty-focused arguments clash in a real or hypothetical project?

Summary

  • Utilitarianism evaluates outcomes; deontology emphasizes duties and rights; virtue ethics focuses on character; care ethics centers relationships and vulnerability.
  • No single theory solves every case; professionals use them as lenses.
  • Structured decision frameworks make reasoning transparent and reviewable.
  • Theory supports the multi-perspective analysis used across this course.
