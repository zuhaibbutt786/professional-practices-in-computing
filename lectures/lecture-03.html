Ethical Theories & Decision Frameworks

Utilitarianism, deontology, virtue ethics, and practical frameworks for professional decision-making in computing.

Module 03~50–70 minCore Theory

Learning Objectives

  • Explain the core ideas of utilitarianism, deontology, and virtue ethics in plain language.
  • Apply at least one structured decision framework to a computing dilemma.
  • Identify strengths and limits of each theory when applied to software, data, and AI contexts.
  • Recognize when theories conflict and how professionals navigate residual judgment.

Introduction

Professional codes rest on deeper ethical traditions. When a code is silent or principles clash, computing professionals need the ability to reason from first principles. This lecture introduces the major ethical theories used in technology ethics and shows how to turn them into practical decision aids.

Motivation

Imagine a ranking algorithm that improves average user engagement but systematically disadvantages a minority group. Utilitarian reasoning may favor the aggregate gain; deontological reasoning may forbid treating people as mere means; virtue ethics asks what a responsible practitioner would do. Without a framework, teams default to commercial pressure or gut feeling.

Real Story

Classic trolley-problem variants reappear in autonomous vehicle design, content moderation, and medical triage algorithms. Engineers who only optimize metrics without ethical framing repeatedly produce systems that later require costly remediation and public apology.

Core Theories

Utilitarianism

Evaluate actions by their consequences for overall well-being. Useful for risk analysis and impact assessment; weak when harms are concentrated on the few or when rights are ignored.

Deontology

Focus on duties, rights, and rules that must be respected regardless of outcomes. Strong for privacy, consent, and non-discrimination; can be rigid when rules conflict.

Virtue Ethics

Ask what a person of good character and professional excellence would do. Emphasizes judgment, honesty, and courage; less prescriptive for novel cases.

Practical Frameworks

Many organizations combine impact analysis, stakeholder mapping, rights checks, and escalation paths. Frameworks do not eliminate judgment; they structure it.

Multi-Perspective Notes

  • AI: Fairness metrics often encode utilitarian trade-offs; rights-based constraints are needed as hard limits.
  • Data Science: Secondary use of data raises consent and purpose-limitation questions that pure utility analysis underweights.
  • Software Engineering: Safety-critical systems demand both outcome optimization and non-negotiable duties of care.
  • Cybersecurity: Responsible disclosure balances public benefit against potential harm from premature exposure.

Knowledge Check

Reflection

  1. When have you seen a metric-driven decision conflict with a rights-based concern?
  2. Which ethical theory feels most natural to you, and what does that reveal about your blind spots?

Assignment

Select a recent technology controversy. Analyze it under utilitarianism, deontology, and virtue ethics (300–400 words each). Conclude with a recommended professional response (150 words).

Cheat Sheet

  • Utilitarianism → consequences / aggregate welfare
  • Deontology → duties, rights, rules
  • Virtue ethics → character and professional excellence
  • Frameworks structure judgment; they do not replace it

Glossary

Utilitarianism
Ethical theory that judges actions by their consequences for overall well-being.
Deontology
Ethical theory centered on duties and rules independent of outcomes.
Virtue ethics
Approach focused on the character traits of a good professional.
