Ethical Theories & Decision-Making Frameworks

Classical ethical theories applied to computing, AI, data, and cybersecurity dilemmas.

Module 03 ~50–65 min Core Theory

Learning Objectives

  • Summarize utilitarianism, deontology, and virtue ethics in the context of computing decisions.
  • Apply at least one structured ethical decision framework to a realistic professional scenario.
  • Compare how different theories evaluate the same dilemma (privacy vs. security, accuracy vs. fairness).
  • Recognize the limits of pure theory when organizational incentives and incomplete information dominate.
  • Connect ethical theory to ACM/IEEE principles and emerging AI ethics guidance.

Introduction

Professional codes tell you what the profession expects. Ethical theory helps you understand why those expectations exist and how to reason when codes conflict or are silent. Computing professionals routinely face trade-offs: release a model that is mostly accurate but biased against a minority group; log user behavior to improve safety while increasing surveillance risk; delay a security patch to meet a contractual deadline. Theory does not eliminate hard choices, but it supplies disciplined language and criteria.

Motivation

Without shared frameworks, ethical discussion collapses into personal opinion or corporate policy. Students and practitioners need portable tools they can use in design reviews, incident post-mortems, and policy debates.

Real Story: The Trolley Problem Goes Digital

Autonomous vehicle ethics popularized simplified trolley dilemmas. Real systems involve probabilistic harm, distributed responsibility, training-data choices, and regulatory constraints. Classical theories still illuminate the stakes, but they must be adapted to scale, opacity, and multi-agent settings.

Industrial Scenario

Your team trains a hiring screening model. Validation shows higher false-negative rates for candidates from certain demographic groups. Shipping on schedule would improve short-term revenue metrics. Delaying for fairness work risks missing a market window. How would a utilitarian, a deontologist, and a virtue ethicist each approach the decision? What process would you recommend?

Case Study: Bias, Utility, and Duty

Background. A predictive policing tool reduces average response time but concentrates stops in already over-policed neighborhoods.

Problem. Aggregate utility may rise while specific groups bear disproportionate costs.

Ethical issue. Is the relevant unit of analysis the average citizen or the worst-off community?

Legal & business. Discrimination law, public trust, and long-term legitimacy are at stake.

Lessons. Theory helps surface which values are being prioritized; it does not replace stakeholder engagement or empirical measurement of harms.

Core Theories

Utilitarianism

Focus on consequences: maximize overall good (or minimize overall harm). In computing, this often appears as risk-benefit analysis, A/B testing of harm rates, or cost-benefit of safety features. Challenges include measurement of “utility,” distribution of harms, and long-term vs. short-term effects.

Deontology

Focus on duties and rules: certain actions are right or wrong regardless of outcome. Privacy as a side-constraint, informed consent as a duty, and prohibitions on deception fit this family. Challenges include conflicting duties and rigid application in novel situations.

Virtue Ethics

Focus on character and professional excellence: what would a responsible, honest, careful computing professional do? Emphasizes habits, role models, and organizational culture. Challenges include vagueness and cultural variation in virtues.

Practical frameworks

Many organizations combine elements: identify stakeholders, list options, evaluate consequences and duties, check consistency with codes, document reasoning, and revisit after deployment.

Multi-Perspective Notes

AI

Value alignment and reward design are utilitarian in spirit; constitutional or rule-based constraints are deontological.

Data Science

Fairness metrics operationalize different moral theories (equality of opportunity vs. equalized odds, etc.).

Cybersecurity

Duty to protect often conflicts with transparency and research disclosure norms.

Software Engineering

Safety-critical practice leans toward strict duties and conservative release criteria.

Knowledge Check

Reflection Questions

  1. When should consequences outweigh duties in a professional computing decision?
  2. Can virtue ethics guide concrete design choices, or is it mainly about culture?
  3. How would you document an ethical analysis so that a future auditor can understand it?

Assignment

Select one real or realistic computing dilemma (privacy, bias, dual-use, safety). Analyze it under utilitarianism, deontology, and virtue ethics (250–300 words each). Conclude with a recommended decision and a short process for ongoing monitoring (200 words).

Cheat Sheet

  • Utilitarianism → outcomes and aggregate welfare.
  • Deontology → duties, rights, side-constraints.
  • Virtue ethics → character, habits, professional excellence.
  • Frameworks combine identification of stakeholders, options, duties, consequences, and documentation.

Glossary

Utilitarianism
Ethical theory judging actions by their consequences for overall good.
Deontology
Ethical theory emphasizing duties and rules independent of outcomes.
Virtue ethics
Ethical approach centered on character traits and professional excellence.
Side-constraint
A moral limit that may not be violated even to produce better consequences.
