Ethical Theories & Decision-Making Frameworks

Classical ethical theories and practical decision frameworks that help computing professionals reason systematically about hard choices under uncertainty.

⏱ 85–100 min 📚 Core 🎯 Reasoning ✍️ Frameworks + Code
🎯

Learning Objectives

By the end of this lecture you will be able to:

  • Explain the core claims of utilitarianism, deontology, virtue ethics, and care ethics.
  • Apply each theory to a concrete computing dilemma and identify where they agree or conflict.
  • Use structured decision-making frameworks (stakeholder analysis, ethical impact steps) in professional contexts.
  • Recognize the strengths and limitations of theory-driven versus case-driven ethical reasoning.
  • Translate high-level ethical considerations into concrete process or design checks (illustrated with simple code patterns).
📖

Introduction

Professional codes tell you what the community values. Ethical theories help you reason about why those values matter and how to proceed when values conflict. In computing, conflicts are common: accuracy versus fairness, innovation speed versus safety, personalization versus privacy, transparency versus security.

This lecture introduces the major families of ethical theory used in professional practice and pairs them with practical decision frameworks you can actually apply in design reviews, incident responses, and product decisions.

🔥

Motivation

Relying only on intuition or “what the code says” leaves you unprepared when the situation is novel. Theories and frameworks give you:

  • A shared vocabulary for discussing disagreements inside a team.
  • A way to surface hidden assumptions (about who counts, what counts as harm, what time horizon matters).
  • A defense against purely self-serving rationalizations.
  • A bridge between abstract principles and concrete design or process choices.
⚖️

Major Ethical Theories

1. Utilitarianism (Consequentialism)

An action is right if it produces the greatest overall good (or least overall harm) for the greatest number. In computing this often appears as cost-benefit or risk-benefit analysis: “Will deploying this model reduce more harm than it creates?”

Strength & Limitation
Strength: forces attention to actual outcomes and affected populations.
Limitation: hard to measure and compare different kinds of harm; can justify harming a minority for a large majority gain; ignores duties and rights that should not be traded.

2. Deontology (Duty-Based Ethics)

Some actions are right or wrong in themselves, regardless of consequences. Duties, rights, and rules matter. Classic formulations emphasize treating persons as ends, never merely as means, and acting only on maxims you could will as universal law.

In computing: “We do not collect this category of data without meaningful consent, even if it would improve model performance.” Or “We do not ship known critical vulnerabilities to meet a release date.”

3. Virtue Ethics

Focuses on the character of the agent rather than isolated acts or outcomes. What would a responsible, honest, courageous, just computing professional do? Emphasizes habits, practical wisdom, and long-term professional identity.

Useful for culture questions: “Does our team’s normal way of working cultivate carefulness and truth-telling, or corner-cutting and silence?”

4. Care Ethics & Relational Approaches

Emphasizes relationships, interdependence, and responsibilities that arise from particular connections (to users, colleagues, communities, future maintainers). Especially relevant to accessibility, inclusive design, and long-term stewardship of systems that affect vulnerable groups.

Comparing the Lenses

Utilitarian question

“What overall consequences (benefits and harms) are likely, for whom, over what time horizon?”

Deontological question

“Are we violating any duty, right, or professional rule that should not be traded away?”

Virtue question

“What kind of professional or organization are we becoming by acting this way?”

Care question

“Who depends on us, and what do our relationships require in this situation?”

Practical Decision-Making Frameworks

Theories alone do not decide cases. Practitioners need structured processes. Here are two widely useful frameworks.

Framework A — Stakeholder & Impact Analysis

  1. Identify stakeholders (including non-users and future parties).
  2. For each, list potential benefits, harms, rights, and power differences.
  3. Assess uncertainty and distribution of impacts.
  4. Generate options (including “do not build / do not deploy”).
  5. Evaluate options under multiple ethical lenses.
  6. Choose, document rationale, and plan monitoring.

Framework B — Professional Ethical Analysis Steps

  1. Clarify the facts and the decision that must be made.
  2. Identify applicable professional duties and organizational policies.
  3. Surface conflicts among duties, values, or stakeholders.
  4. Consider alternative actions and their likely consequences.
  5. Test the leading option: publicity test, reversibility, virtue, rights.
  6. Decide, implement with safeguards, and review after the fact.

Illustrative Code Examples

The following snippets are not production recommendations. They illustrate how ethical considerations can be made explicit in process and design. Language is deliberately simple and secondary to the professional idea.

Example 1 — Encoding a simple ethical checklist (pseudocode)

function ethicalReview(feature):
    stakeholders = identifyStakeholders(feature)
    harms = estimatePotentialHarms(feature, stakeholders)
    duties  = applicableProfessionalDuties(feature)

    if any(harm.severity == "critical" and harm.mitigation == None):
        return REJECT("Critical unmitigated harm")

    if violatesHardDuty(duties):          # e.g., no meaningful consent
        return REJECT("Hard professional duty violated")

    if netExpectedValue(feature) < 0:    # rough utilitarian screen
        return REVISE("Negative expected impact")

    return APPROVE_WITH_MONITORING(feature)

Illustrates a hybrid screen: deontological hard constraints first, then a consequentialist net-impact check, plus mandatory monitoring.

Example 2 — Making consent and purpose explicit (conceptual)

# Conceptual data-handling guard (not a library recommendation)
def process_user_data(record, purpose):
    if purpose not in record.allowed_purposes:
        raise PermissionError("Purpose not covered by consent")

    if record.requires_minimization:
        record = minimize_fields(record, purpose)

    audit_log(actor="system", action="process", 
              purpose=purpose, data_id=record.id)
    return transform(record, purpose)

Shows purpose limitation and auditability — two concrete expressions of respect for persons and accountability.

Example 3 — Decision record for transparency

decision_record = {
    "decision": "Deploy model v2.3 to 10% traffic",
    "date": "2026-08-07",
    "ethical_lenses": {
        "utilitarian": "Expected net reduction in false rejects",
        "deontological": "No protected-attribute direct use; appeal path exists",
        "virtue": "Team documented residual risk instead of hiding it"
    },
    "residual_risks": ["Possible disparate impact on group X"],
    "monitoring": ["weekly fairness dashboard", "user appeal volume"],
    "owner": "model-governance@example.com"
}

A lightweight decision record that makes ethical reasoning reviewable — supporting both accountability and organizational learning.

Case Study — Conflicting Lenses on a Ranking System

A platform ranks content for user feeds. Optimizing purely for engagement (a utilitarian-style metric of “time spent / satisfaction”) can amplify polarizing or harmful content. A deontological stance might insist on hard limits (no amplification of certain categories regardless of engagement). A virtue perspective asks what kind of information environment the company is habitually creating. A care perspective focuses on users who are more vulnerable to manipulation or distress.

Discussion points:

  • Where do the lenses agree? Where do they force a choice?
  • Which lens is most often missing in pure engagement-driven product cultures?
  • How would you structure a decision process that forces all four lenses to be considered?

Industrial Scenario

Scenario: The Accuracy–Fairness Trade-off

Your team’s new risk model improves overall accuracy by 4% but increases false-positive rates for one demographic group by a noticeable margin. Business stakeholders want the accuracy gain. Advocacy groups and some internal reviewers raise fairness concerns. Legal says current regulation does not clearly prohibit the change.

Apply the frameworks:

  • Map stakeholders and impacts.
  • Identify hard duties versus tradeable considerations.
  • Generate at least three options beyond “ship” / “don’t ship”.
  • Record which ethical lenses support each option.

Reflection Questions

1. Which ethical theory feels most natural to you? Which feels most uncomfortable? What does that reveal about your default professional reasoning?

2. Describe a recent technical decision (real or hypothetical) where two lenses would have given different recommendations. How would you resolve the conflict?

3. How might you make ethical analysis a lightweight, repeatable habit in a fast-moving team without turning it into bureaucracy?

💬

Discussion Prompts

  1. Can a purely utilitarian organization still be a good professional environment? Why or why not?
  2. “We followed the process” is sometimes used as a defense. When is that sufficient, and when is it not?
  3. How should teams handle genuine, irreducible disagreement among competent professionals applying different ethical lenses?

Assignment & Mini-Project

Assignment 3.1 — Multi-Lens Analysis

Choose one of the following dilemmas (or a real one from your experience):

  • Deploying a generative model that can produce convincing but false information.
  • Using scraped public data to train a commercial model without individual consent.
  • Shipping a security patch that temporarily breaks accessibility features.

Write a 600–800 word analysis that (1) applies at least three ethical lenses, (2) uses one of the decision frameworks, and (3) ends with a justified recommendation and monitoring plan.

Mini-Project — Personal Decision Template

Create a one-page personal template (checklist or short form) you will use for non-trivial professional decisions. Include prompts drawn from at least two theories and one structured framework. Keep it practical enough to use under time pressure.

Summary

  • Utilitarianism focuses on overall consequences; deontology on duties and rights; virtue ethics on character; care ethics on relationships.
  • No single theory resolves every computing dilemma; skilled professionals switch and combine lenses deliberately.
  • Structured frameworks turn abstract theory into repeatable professional practice.
  • Simple process and design patterns (checklists, purpose checks, decision records) can make ethical reasoning visible and improvable.
  • The goal is not moral perfection but reliable, reviewable judgment under real constraints.

Glossary

Consequentialism
Family of theories (including utilitarianism) that judge actions primarily by their outcomes.
Deontology
Family of theories that emphasize duties, rights, and rules independent of consequences.
Virtue ethics
Approach that centers the moral character and practical wisdom of the agent.
Stakeholder
Any party that can affect or is affected by a decision or system (including non-users).
Residual risk
Risk that remains after mitigations; responsible practice requires acknowledging and monitoring it.
