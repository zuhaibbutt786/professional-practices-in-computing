Module 03 · Ethical Reasoning

Ethical Theories & Decision Frameworks

Build a rigorous toolkit for analyzing professional dilemmas using classical and contemporary ethical theories, structured decision processes, and multi-stakeholder perspectives relevant to computing, AI, data science, and cybersecurity.

Utilitarianism Deontology Virtue Ethics Care Ethics Decision Frameworks

Learning Objectives

By the end of this module you will be able to:

  • Explain the core claims of major ethical theories (consequentialist, deontological, virtue, care, and justice-based approaches) in plain language.
  • Apply at least two structured ethical decision frameworks to realistic computing scenarios.
  • Compare how different theories yield different recommendations for the same dilemma.
  • Identify stakeholder interests and power asymmetries in technology-mediated harms.
  • Articulate the limits of pure theory when facing incomplete information, conflicting duties, and organizational constraints.
  • Connect ethical analysis to professional codes (ACM/IEEE) and emerging AI governance principles.

Introduction

Professional practice in computing is not merely about writing correct code or shipping features on time. Every system you design, every dataset you process, and every model you deploy encodes value judgments about what matters, whose interests count, and what risks are acceptable. Ethical theories give us shared vocabulary and disciplined methods for making those judgments explicit and open to criticism.

This lecture does not aim to convert you to a single moral philosophy. Instead it equips you with multiple lenses so that you can (1) diagnose why reasonable people disagree, (2) structure your own reasoning under pressure, and (3) communicate ethical analysis clearly to teammates, managers, clients, and regulators.

Motivation: Why Theory Matters in Practice

When a product manager asks “Should we ship this recommendation ranking even though it amplifies outrage?” or a security engineer asks “Is it acceptable to use this zero-day before the vendor patches?” pure intuition is fragile. Theories help you:

  • Surface hidden assumptions (e.g., “maximizing engagement is always good”).
  • Identify missing stakeholders (children, future users, non-users harmed by externalities).
  • Distinguish short-term local benefits from long-term systemic costs.
  • Defend decisions under audit or public scrutiny with coherent reasons rather than after-the-fact rationalizations.

Industry failures from Cambridge Analytica to biased hiring algorithms to unsafe autonomous systems repeatedly show that technical competence without ethical competence is insufficient.

Real Story: The Algorithmic Hiring Filter

A large technology company built an internal recruiting tool that ranked applicants based on historical hiring data. The system systematically down-ranked resumes that contained the word “women’s” (as in “women’s chess club”) and preferred patterns associated with male candidates who had been hired in the past. The team initially framed the problem as a pure optimization and fairness-after-the-fact issue. Only after external criticism and internal ethical review did they recognize that the training data encoded past discrimination, that the system amplified it at scale, and that deontological constraints (do not systematically disadvantage protected groups) and justice considerations required redesign rather than post-hoc score calibration.

The episode illustrates how consequentialist “better predictions” can conflict with non-discrimination duties, and how virtue and organizational culture shape whether engineers feel empowered to raise the issue early.

Industrial Scenario

You are a senior data scientist at a fintech startup. Your model for credit risk improves default prediction by 8% over the previous system, which would reduce portfolio losses. However, the model uses features that are correlated with protected attributes (zip code, device type, time-of-day of application). When you remove those features, performance drops. Compliance asks for a “fairness report.” Product wants the higher-performing model. Legal is concerned about disparate impact claims. Your manager asks for a recommendation by Friday.

This is not a pure technical question. Different ethical theories will weight accuracy gains, individual rights, group fairness metrics, transparency duties, and the firm’s long-term reputation differently. You will need a structured way to present options and trade-offs.

Historical Background

Western ethical theory has deep roots in classical philosophy (Aristotle on virtue and flourishing; Kant on duty and respect for persons; Mill and Bentham on utility). Twentieth-century developments added care ethics (Gilligan, Noddings), justice as fairness (Rawls), and capability approaches (Sen, Nussbaum). Computing-specific work accelerated in the 1980s–1990s with computer ethics (Moor, Johnson, Gotterbarn) and later with value-sensitive design, responsible AI, and AI ethics frameworks from ACM, IEEE, OECD, UNESCO, and national regulators.

Contemporary professional practice draws on this heritage while remaining pragmatic: codes of ethics, risk management frameworks (e.g., NIST AI RMF), and organizational processes that force explicit consideration of harms.

Core Ethical Theories

1. Consequentialism / Utilitarianism

Core idea: The right action is the one that produces the best overall consequences (often understood as maximizing well-being or minimizing harm).

Strengths in computing: Forces attention to downstream effects at scale; useful for safety cases, cost-benefit analysis of security controls, and evaluating system-level impacts.

Weaknesses: Difficult measurement of “utility”; can justify sacrificing individuals for aggregate gain; ignores rights and duties that many consider inviolable; short-term vs long-term utility conflicts.

Computing example: Deciding whether to release a vulnerability disclosure that causes short-term pain for some vendors but reduces systemic risk for millions of users.

2. Deontology (Duty-Based Ethics)

Core idea: Certain actions are right or wrong regardless of consequences; persons must be treated as ends, not merely as means (Kantian formulation).

Strengths: Protects individual rights (privacy, informed consent, non-discrimination); supports bright-line rules (do not deceive users; do not hide known critical vulnerabilities from affected parties).

Weaknesses: Duties can conflict; rigid rules may produce bad outcomes in edge cases; hard to prioritize when multiple duties apply.

Computing example: Refusing to implement dark patterns even if they increase conversion, because they treat users as means to revenue.

3. Virtue Ethics

Core idea: Focus on the character of the moral agent—what a good professional would do, what habits of excellence (honesty, courage, care, justice, humility) look like in practice.

Strengths: Emphasizes professional identity and culture; useful for mentoring and organizational design; flexible across novel situations.

Weaknesses: Less precise action guidance; can be culturally relative; hard to operationalize in compliance checklists.

Computing example: Cultivating the habit of documenting assumptions and residual risks rather than shipping with silent uncertainty.

4. Care Ethics

Core idea: Moral significance of relationships, interdependence, and attending to the concrete needs of particular others (especially the vulnerable).

Strengths: Highlights power asymmetries and relational harms that aggregate metrics miss; valuable for accessibility, user research, and content moderation contexts.

Weaknesses: Risk of partiality; scale challenges when millions of users are involved.

Computing example: Designing content moderation that prioritizes protection of minors and marginalized communities even when it reduces engagement metrics.

5. Justice and Fairness Approaches

Includes distributive justice (how benefits and burdens are allocated), procedural justice (fair processes), and recognition justice. Rawlsian “veil of ignorance” thought experiments and capability approaches are influential. In machine learning this maps onto debates about group fairness metrics, individual fairness, and equality of opportunity versus equality of outcome.

Structured Decision Frameworks

Theories alone are not enough under time pressure. Practitioners need repeatable processes.

Framework A: Stakeholder Impact Analysis

  1. Identify affected parties (users, non-users, employees, partners, society, future generations).
  2. Map benefits, risks, and rights for each.
  3. Assess power imbalances and ability to consent or exit.
  4. Check consistency with professional codes and law.
  5. Document residual risks and monitoring plans.

Framework B: Ethical Decision Procedure (adapted from common professional models)

  1. Recognize the ethical dimension (not just a technical or business issue).
  2. Gather facts and clarify uncertainty.
  3. Identify stakeholders and their legitimate interests.
  4. Generate options (including “do nothing” and “escalate”).
  5. Evaluate options through multiple ethical lenses.
  6. Choose, implement, and monitor; be prepared to revise.
  7. Reflect and share lessons (within confidentiality constraints).

Framework C: Value-Sensitive Design (high-level)

Integrate human values (privacy, autonomy, trust, accountability, sustainability) into design from the earliest stages through conceptual, empirical, and technical investigations.

Multi-Perspective Analysis

Professional Perspective

Professionals are expected to exercise independent judgment, not merely follow orders. Codes require prioritizing public interest, honesty, and competence. Decision frameworks help you justify when you must push back or escalate.

Ethical Perspective

No single theory is authoritative in all cases. Responsible practice often involves “ethical triangulation”: if consequentialist, deontological, and virtue analyses all converge, confidence increases; if they diverge, deeper deliberation and stakeholder engagement are warranted.

Legal Perspective

Law sets minimum floors (anti-discrimination, privacy statutes, product liability, AI-specific rules in some jurisdictions). Ethical analysis often goes beyond legal compliance. Conversely, some legal requirements may themselves raise ethical questions (e.g., mandatory data retention).

Business Perspective

Short-term profit can conflict with long-term trust and license to operate. Ethical failures create regulatory, reputational, and talent risks. Framing ethical analysis in terms of risk management and sustainable value creation often improves organizational uptake.

AI Perspective

AI systems amplify scale, opacity, and autonomy of decision-making. Theories of responsibility (who is accountable when a model harms?) and principles of transparency, fairness, and human oversight become critical. Frameworks such as NIST AI RMF operationalize risk-based ethical reasoning.

Data Science Perspective

Data work embeds choices about measurement, inclusion/exclusion, labeling, and optimization targets. Each choice has ethical content. Documentation (datasheets, model cards) and participatory practices help surface those choices.

Software Engineering Perspective

Requirements engineering, architecture decisions, testing criteria, and deployment gates are natural insertion points for ethical checkpoints. “Ethics as a non-functional requirement” is an incomplete but useful framing when combined with process.

Cybersecurity Perspective

Security ethics involves dual-use research, coordinated disclosure, active defense boundaries, and the tension between user privacy and threat detection. Deontological constraints (do not cause unjustified harm) and consequentialist harm reduction both apply.

Case Study: Content Ranking and Amplification

Background: A social platform’s ranking algorithm optimizes for time-on-site and engagement. Internal research shows that content that provokes strong emotional reactions (including anger and outrage) is preferentially amplified. External researchers link the system to polarization and real-world harm.

Problem: Engagement metrics conflict with societal well-being and user mental health.

Ethical issues: Consequentialist harm at scale; deontological questions about manipulating attention; care ethics concerns for vulnerable users; justice questions about whose voices are amplified.

Legal / regulatory: Platform liability regimes vary; emerging online safety legislation; potential advertising and consumer protection issues.

Business impact: Changing the objective function may reduce short-term engagement and revenue while reducing regulatory and reputational risk.

Technical lessons: Optimization targets are value-laden; measurement of “harm” is difficult but necessary; A/B tests can miss long-term and societal effects.

Professional lessons: Engineers and data scientists share responsibility for downstream effects of ranking systems; escalation channels and ethical review boards matter; transparency with the public is part of accountability.

Discussion prompts:

  • Which ethical theory most strongly supports changing the ranking objective? Which supports keeping engagement primary?
  • What would a virtue-ethics culture look like inside the ranking team?
  • How should the company weigh evidence that is correlational rather than causal?

Assignment

Individual written analysis (800–1200 words)

Select one real or realistic computing dilemma (hiring algorithm, content moderation, medical AI triage, security disclosure, environmental cost of training, etc.).

  1. Describe the facts and uncertainty clearly.
  2. Identify at least four stakeholder groups and their interests.
  3. Analyze the situation through at least two ethical theories and one structured decision framework.
  4. State your recommended course of action and the residual risks you accept.
  5. Explain how you would monitor and revise the decision.

Submit as a PDF or Markdown file. Cite any sources. Original analysis only.

Cheat Sheet

  • Utilitarianism: Best overall consequences / well-being.
  • Deontology: Duties and respect for persons; rights matter independently of outcomes.
  • Virtue ethics: Character and professional excellence.
  • Care ethics: Relationships, vulnerability, concrete needs.
  • Justice: Fair distribution, process, and recognition.
  • Practice: Stakeholder analysis + multi-lens evaluation + document & monitor.
  • Key habit: Make value judgments explicit rather than burying them in metrics.

Glossary

Consequentialism
Moral theories that evaluate actions solely by their outcomes.
Deontology
Duty-based ethics; some actions are right or wrong regardless of consequences.
Virtue ethics
Focus on the moral character and habits of the agent.
Care ethics
Emphasizes relationships, interdependence, and attending to particular others.
Stakeholder
Any party affected by or able to affect a decision or system.
Value-sensitive design
Methodology that accounts for human values throughout the design process.
Dual-use
Technology or research that can be used for beneficial and harmful purposes.

Summary

Ethical theories provide disciplined ways to reason about professional dilemmas in computing. Utilitarianism, deontology, virtue ethics, care ethics, and justice approaches each illuminate different aspects of a problem. Structured frameworks turn theory into repeatable practice under time and organizational pressure. Multi-perspective analysis (professional, legal, business, AI, data science, software engineering, cybersecurity) prevents narrow framing. The goal is not philosophical purity but better, more accountable decisions and the ability to explain them.
