Module 04 · AI Ethics

Responsible AI & Algorithmic Systems

Fairness, accountability, transparency, risk management, and the professional obligations of those who design, train, deploy, and oversee AI and data-driven systems.

⏱ ~90–120 min📚 Principles + governance🎯 Responsible AI
🎯

Learning Objectives

  • Articulate core principles of responsible AI (fairness, accountability, transparency, safety, human oversight).
  • Identify common sources of bias and harm in data and models.
  • Apply high-level risk-management thinking to AI systems (context, impact, monitoring).
  • Connect professional codes and emerging frameworks (e.g., NIST AI RMF concepts, OECD principles) to daily practice.
  • Discuss governance roles: who decides objectives, who is accountable when systems fail.
💡

Why Responsible AI

AI systems encode objectives, data histories, and design choices that affect people’s opportunities, safety, and rights. Failures are not only technical; they are often failures of problem framing, evaluation, deployment context, and accountability. Professional practice requires treating these as first-class concerns throughout the lifecycle.

📐

Key Principles

  • Fairness / non-discrimination — avoid unjustified disparate impacts; examine metrics and group outcomes carefully.
  • Accountability — clear ownership of decisions about objectives, data, and deployment; ability to explain and remedy.
  • Transparency & explainability — appropriate disclosure of purpose, limitations, and behavior to users and overseers.
  • Safety & robustness — design and test for failure modes; monitor in production.
  • Human oversight — meaningful human control where stakes are high.
  • Privacy & data governance — respect data-protection principles in training and inference.
🔄

Lifecycle View

Problem definition → data collection & labeling → model development & evaluation → deployment → monitoring & update → decommissioning. Ethical and professional risks appear at every stage. Evaluation must include performance on relevant subgroups and in realistic operating conditions, not only average metrics on a test set.

🔍

Illustrative Cases

Hiring and lending algorithms that reproduced historical bias. Facial recognition with uneven error rates across demographic groups. Content recommendation systems optimizing engagement at the cost of other social goods. Healthcare models trained on non-representative data. Each case shows that “the model was accurate on average” is not a complete professional defense.

✅

Knowledge Check

Responsible AI practice emphasizes:
Maximizing model accuracy on a single held-out test set and shipping.
Considering fairness, accountability, transparency, safety, and context throughout the system lifecycle, with clear ownership of decisions.
Avoiding any use of machine learning because of risk.
Relying solely on post-hoc explanations after harm occurs.
📋

Summary

  • AI systems require professional attention to fairness, accountability, transparency, and safety.
  • Risk depends on context and impact; high-stakes domains demand stronger oversight and evidence.
  • Governance and documentation are part of engineering work, not optional extras.
